**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of July, though the most recent Altos weekly read showed new pending sales at 85,813 — down 3.0% year-over-year, snapping a streak that had run 55 consecutive weeks in positive territory on the 13-week average. That 13-week average still stands at +4.8% YoY after 14 rising weeks, and the 4-week average remains positive at +4.4% YoY, so the weekly dip reads more like seasonal softening than a trend break — but it is worth watching whether it persists into August. Inventory continued to firm, with Altos total active supply at roughly 1.075 million units, up about 1.2% YoY. New listings on the 4-week average ticked up 0.4% YoY, though the most recent weekly print fell 7.1% YoY — the 13-week average at +0.7% YoY tells the cleaner story: supply growth has not accelerated meaningfully. Pending days on market at 70.9 days are up 6.7% YoY, a signal that homes are sitting slightly longer than a year ago, consistent with a market that is moving but not racing. NAR existing sales for June came in at 4.09 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a notable margin, and up 4.1% YoY — the YoY rate is accelerating. On a NSA basis, the June read of 422,000 was +7.9% YoY, corroborating the SAAR strength. New home sales told a quieter story at 628,000 SAAR in June, down 5.1% YoY, with median new home prices falling 2.7% YoY — builders are actively using price to move inventory, and the months-supply of new homes at 9.3 remains elevated. The 30-year fixed rate ended July at 6.83%, ticking up 6 basis points over the month after a period of gradual decline — a reversal worth noting. The spread to the 10-year Treasury sits at 2.13 percentage points, well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points; the 4-week average has been flat for three weeks. If credit conditions normalize and the spread compresses toward historical levels, mortgage rates could fall meaningfully even without a Fed move — that remains the most underappreciated affordability lever in this market. MBA purchase applications for the week of July 24 were +2.7% YoY on a spot basis, but the 4-week average has slipped to -1.4% YoY after four consecutive weeks of decline. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA by two to four weeks — reversed higher to +3.5% on the week of June 26 and the 4-week average turned positive as well, though the 13-week average remains down 2.4% YoY after seven weeks of decline. The divergence between the MII uptick and the MBA softness will be the leading indicator to track in August; if the MII improvement carries through, purchase applications should firm in the back half of the month. Housing starts beat expectations in June at 1,427,000 SAAR — but almost entirely on a jump in multifamily; single-family starts fell 3.2% YoY and are down three consecutive months. Meanwhile, single-family permits at 872,000 SAAR just turned positive YoY for the first time since May 2024, a modest forward signal for single-family supply later in the year, even as total permits missed consensus and turned negative YoY.

The macro backdrop in July was, on balance, more favorable for housing than the headlines suggested. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — above the consensus estimate of 59,000 — and the unemployment rate fell to 4.2%, again well clear of the 4.4% expected. A healthy labor market keeps the buyer pool intact; what keeps us watching is the long-term unemployed cohort, which reached 1,937,000 in June, up 17.3% YoY. That cohort does not affect the current selling season but tends to lead delinquency cycles by six to twelve months — it is the number to check each month even when everything else looks fine. The bigger macro story in July was inflation. Headline CPI for June came in at -0.4% month-over-month against a consensus expectation of +0.9%, and core CPI was essentially flat month-over-month against an expected +0.3%. Core PCE for June confirmed the picture at +0.1% MoM versus an expected +0.3%. The mechanism for housing: softer-than-expected inflation reduces the pressure on the Fed to hold rates elevated, which over time should allow Treasury yields — and through them, mortgage rates — to ease. The 10-year yield ended July at 4.70%, having ticked back up late in the month, which is why the mortgage rate reversed higher on the final day despite the good inflation news; bond markets are still digesting the data.
