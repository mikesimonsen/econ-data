**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive over the four-week window ending June 26, but the internal picture got more complicated. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales sat at +4.81% YoY — now 55 consecutive weeks of positive year-over-year readings — and the 4-week average remained constructive at +4.37% YoY. The spot week, however, slipped to -2.99% YoY, the third consecutive weekly pullback, which is the number to watch rather than dismiss. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, confirming that the pace of absorption has quietly been softening all spring. Inventory rose for an eighth consecutive week to roughly 1.07 million active listings, up about 1.2% YoY — growth that has decelerated noticeably from the 10-15% YoY clips we saw a year ago. New listings flipped negative on a spot basis (-7.1% YoY) while the 4-week average remained barely positive at +0.4% YoY; sellers appear to be pulling back into the summer in a pattern that looks more like 2019 than the inventory-flush environment of 2023-2024. NAR's June existing home sales print — 4.09 million SAAR, up 4.1% YoY — beat expectations of 3.85 million handily, and the NSA read of 422,000 units was up 7.9% from a year ago, the strongest YoY gain in several months. The caveat is that the SAAR figure was itself a monthly step-down, and new home sales for May came in at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.4% YoY, with months supply on new homes at 10.3 — elevated enough that builders are clearly managing through existing pipeline rather than reaching for new ground. Single-family completions hit their lowest level since June 2011, down 17.4% YoY, which means the resale market is not getting a supply relief valve from builder deliveries any time soon. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.68% today, ticking up four basis points in recent sessions after a modest decline through late June. The more important number remains the spread to the 10-year Treasury: at roughly 209 basis points, it is still running about 39 basis points above the long-run historical norm of around 170. That gap is the hidden affordability story — if it compresses toward historical norms as credit-market volatility fades, the 30-year rate could fall meaningfully even without any change in the 10-year yield. MBA purchase applications for the week ending July 10 were down 1.5% YoY, and the 4-week average has just ticked negative for the first time in over a year at -0.6% YoY — a threshold worth flagging even though the 13-week average remains solidly positive at +5.2%. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index, which leads MBA applications by two to four weeks, had its 4-week average reverse upward to +2.3% on its own level — but remains down 2.3% YoY, diverging from the still-positive MBA 13-week trend. When MII is softening YoY while MBA holds firm, it typically resolves toward MII. Watch the MBA 4-week average over the next four weeks; if it extends below -1%, the Altos pending trend will follow.

The macro picture shifted more than the housing data did this month. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — solid enough — and the unemployment rate fell a tenth to 4.2%, beating a consensus of 4.4%. That beat matters for sentiment at listing appointments: employed buyers with confidence can and do transact. The complication is the labor market's texture. The hires rate held at 3.3% while the quits rate remained at 1.9%, both below year-ago levels, signaling a labor market where people are staying put rather than moving toward opportunity — and voluntary moves are one of the cleaner proxies for housing demand. The long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) ticked down modestly in June but remains 17.3% above year-ago levels; it does not affect this selling season, but that accumulation is the early input to a distressed-borrower story 6-12 months from now. On inflation, June CPI came in sharply cooler than expected — headline at -0.4% month-over-month against a +0.9% consensus, core at essentially flat against a +0.3% consensus. Shelter CPI continued rising, up 3.3% YoY, a lagged signal that private-market rent data has already been predicting a deceleration. Core PCE for May was still running at 3.4% YoY, above the Fed's target, which is why rate cuts remain a 2026 later-story rather than an imminent catalyst. The CPI print does, however, put modest downward pressure on the 10-year yield and, through that channel, on the mortgage-Treasury spread if credit conditions remain calm.
