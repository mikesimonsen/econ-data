**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through the spring, but the most recent Altos weekly read introduced a note of caution. New pending sales came in at 85,813 for the week ending June 26 — down 3.0% year-over-year, snapping what had been a positive YoY streak — though the four-week average remains up 4.4% YoY and the 13-week average sits at +4.8% YoY, now 55 consecutive weeks in positive territory. That divergence between the spot read and the smoothed trend is the right frame here: a single week of negative YoY pending sales in late June is normal noise as the holiday calendar distorts weekly counts, and the durable trend still points to modest demand growth. Inventory on the Altos platform reached 1.07 million active listings as of June 26, up 1.2% YoY — growth is positive but clearly decelerating from the double-digit YoY expansions we saw in 2024. New listings came in at 90,746 for the week, down 7.1% YoY on the spot read, though the four-week average is still slightly positive at +0.4% YoY and the 13-week average has been rising for 14 consecutive weeks. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking longer to go under contract than a year ago, which is consistent with buyers having more choices. The NAR May existing home sales print at 4.17 million SAAR beat expectations by a wide margin (consensus was 3.85M), up 3.2% YoY with YoY acceleration — that is a strong number and one that does not fully square with the softer weekly Altos reads that preceded it; the June report, due in mid-July, will be the reconciliation. New home sales diverged notably: the May Census print came in at 580K SAAR, down 7.5% YoY, with months supply jumping to 10.3 — builders are sitting on elevated finished inventory and pulling back, as single-family starts fell for a second consecutive month and are now down 7.1% YoY. The 30-year fixed rate closed July 7 at 6.63%, ticking up four basis points over the past week after a brief decline — the spread to the 10-year Treasury sits at 2.14 percentage points, still about 44 basis points above the long-run historical norm of roughly 1.7pp. That excess spread remains the hidden affordability lever: if it compresses toward 1.8–1.9pp as lender capacity catches up to origination volume, the 30-year would fall toward 6.3–6.4% without any Fed action. The MBA purchase index four-week average is up only 0.5% YoY — decelerating sharply from the +5% pace on the 13-week average — and the most recent weekly read turned negative YoY for the first time in over a year. The Xactus MII, which leads MBA by two to four weeks, reversed higher on its four-week average (+2.3% period over period), suggesting the MBA softness may be transient rather than the start of a trend. Watch the next two Altos weekly prints and the July MBA reads: if the MII uptick translates as it historically has, purchase applications should stabilize in mid-July.

The macro backdrop shifted modestly in housing's favor this month, though not without complications. June payrolls came in at roughly 159K — a solid number — and the unemployment rate fell a tenth to 4.2%, beating a consensus expecting 4.4%. For housing, an unemployment rate that holds below 4.5% keeps the distressed-seller pipeline contained; forced listings remain structurally low, which is part of why inventory growth has decelerated. The complicating piece is inflation: Core PCE for May printed at 3.41% YoY, above the Fed's 2% target, and the May CPI shelter component — which feeds directly into Core PCE with a lag — was still running at 3.35% YoY. That shelter persistence is why rate cuts remain off the table for now, and why the 10-year Treasury drifted back to 4.59% this week. The JOLTS quits rate held at 1.9%, well below pre-pandemic norms; workers are not confident enough to leave jobs voluntarily, which correlates with reduced household formation risk-taking and suppressed move-up demand. The one signal worth watching closely is the long-term unemployed cohort: 1.937 million workers have been unemployed 27 weeks or more, up 17.3% YoY. That number ticked down this month for the first time in several months, which is welcome — but the YoY accumulation matters more than the monthly wiggle. A cohort of that size, if it grows further, begins to generate mortgage delinquency pressure in the six-to-twelve month window; it is not a current-selling-season story, but agents working with buyers who are recently re-employed should be attentive to lender underwriting tightening before it shows up in headlines.
