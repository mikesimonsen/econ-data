**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of the four weeks, though it decelerated into late June. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales sits at +4.81% year-over-year — a streak now 55 weeks long — while the 4-week average has pulled back to +4.37% YoY, signaling that the pace of outperformance is slowing. The most recent weekly read (week of June 26) went negative YoY at -2.99%, which snapped what had been a solid positive run; one week does not reverse a 55-week trend, but it warrants watching. Inventory rose for an eighth consecutive week to roughly 1.075 million active listings, up about 1.2% YoY — growth that is real but moderate, not the kind of supply wave that would tip pricing power to buyers. The new-listings 4-week average turned back up (+0.41% YoY) after a brief dip, and the 13-week average has now risen for 14 consecutive weeks, suggesting sellers are gradually returning. Pending days on market extended to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, which tells you homes are taking longer to reach contract than they did a year ago — a quiet affordability signal. NAR's June existing sales print (4.09 million SAAR) beat consensus of 3.85 million by a wide margin and was up 4.1% YoY on a seasonally adjusted basis; the NSA read confirms the direction at +7.9% YoY. That said, the month-over-month SAAR figure pulled back 1.9% from May, and the beat partly reflects how depressed expectations had become. New home sales told a different story: the May print came in at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.4% YoY, with months of supply at 10.3 — elevated enough that builders are working off existing inventory rather than adding to it, which the permits data confirms. June single-family permits fell 2.4% month-over-month and remain essentially flat YoY (+0.6%), while the June starts headline looked strong (+19% MoM, beating consensus) largely on a 76% jump in 5+ unit starts — multifamily catching up on a delayed pipeline, not a signal of accelerating single-family construction. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.71% today, up 8 basis points over the past month and reversing a brief decline. The 30-10 spread is 2.15 percentage points — still roughly 45 basis points above the long-run norm of around 1.7 points — meaning rates remain artificially elevated relative to Treasuries. If lenders allow that spread to compress toward historical levels, the 30-year could fall to roughly 6.25-6.30% without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications (4-week average) turned negative YoY for the first time in 74 weeks, down 0.6%; the 13-week average is still +5.2% YoY, so the structural trend is intact but losing momentum. The Xactus MII — which typically leads MBA by 2-4 weeks — bounced off its recent low (4-week average up 2.3%), suggesting the MBA softness may be near a floor rather than the start of a sustained decline. Watch the next two MBA weekly prints for confirmation.

The macro backdrop shifted notably this month. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — solid, above the 59,000 consensus — and the unemployment rate fell to 4.2%, beating expectations of 4.4%. That combination keeps job-loss-driven forced selling off the table for the near term and supports household formation. The caveat is the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) cohort, which is 17.3% above year-ago levels even after a modest dip in June; this group typically takes 6-12 months to cycle into mortgage distress, so it does not affect the current selling season but is worth tracking as a leading indicator for delinquency volume heading into 2027. On inflation, the June CPI reading was the standout data point of the month: headline CPI fell 0.4% month-over-month (consensus had expected +0.9%), and core CPI was essentially flat at -0.02% MoM versus +0.3% expected. Shelter CPI rose only 0.12% MoM and is running at 3.26% YoY, still above target but clearly decelerating. Core PCE for May was stickier at +3.41% YoY, so the Fed is not yet at the finish line, but the June CPI data meaningfully reduces the probability of near-term rate hikes and keeps a late-2026 cut in play. The mechanism for housing is straightforward: cooler inflation reads tend to pull the 10-year Treasury yield down, and the 10-year fell before reversing to 4.56% today — a reversal that pushed mortgage rates back up 8 basis points this week.
