**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of the month before softening at the edges. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales sits at +4.81% YoY and has been above zero for 55 consecutive weeks — a streak that reflects a real, if modest, improvement in transaction volume rather than a statistical quirk. The 4-week average cooled to +4.37% YoY, and the most recent weekly read ticked negative at -2.99% YoY, a normal seasonal pattern for late June but worth watching. Inventory rose for eight consecutive weeks, reaching 1.07 million active listings, up 1.2% YoY — growth that has decelerated noticeably from the pace earlier this year. New listings tell a similar story: the 4-week average held marginally positive at +0.41% YoY, but the single-week reading fell 7.1% YoY, and the 13-week average, while still rising, is barely off flat. Supply is not running away from demand. Pending days on market edged up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking a little longer to find a buyer, but not dramatically so. The June NAR print came in at 4.09 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million and up 4.1% YoY, though it ticked down from May's pace — a pattern consistent with a market that is recovering, not accelerating. On NSA terms, June existing sales ran 7.9% above last June, which is the cleaner comparison. New home sales at 628K SAAR diverged meaningfully: down 5.1% YoY, with new home prices (median $398,300, average $475,400) falling sharply as builders continue cutting to clear standing inventory. The 30-year fixed rate is 6.81% as of July 24, effectively unchanged year-over-year. The more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: at 2.11 percentage points, it remains about 40 basis points above the long-run norm of roughly 1.7pp. That gap is compressing slowly — down 27bp from a year ago — and every 10bp of compression is worth roughly a corresponding improvement in effective affordability without any Fed action. Leading indicators are mixed: the MBA purchase index 4-week average is down 1.2% YoY, and the Xactus MII — which tends to lead MBA applications by two to four weeks — showed its 4-week average at -2.30% YoY, though it ticked up from a recent low. The divergence between MII's still-negative YoY trend and MBA's near-flat read is worth tracking: if MII softens again in coming weeks, purchase application volume likely follows. Single-family permits slipped in June but remain barely positive YoY; total permits missed expectations and fell 1.4% YoY. Builders are completing the existing pipeline — single-family completions ran +4.8% YoY in June — while pulling back on future authorizations, particularly in the South, where permits fell 8.7% YoY. That matters more for 2027 supply than the current selling season.

The macro backdrop shifted in one important direction this month: the June CPI print was substantially cooler than expected, with headline running -0.4% month-over-month against an expectation of +0.9%, and core essentially flat against a +0.3% consensus. That is the kind of reading that moves rate expectations, though core PCE — the Fed's preferred measure — remains stickier at +3.41% YoY through May, and shelter CPI continues to run at +3.26% YoY, keeping the Fed's inflation calculus complicated. The mechanism for housing is straightforward: if two more CPI prints come in soft, bond markets will pull the 10-year lower, the spread can do more compression work, and the 30-year rate has a credible path toward the mid-to-upper 6s without any change in Fed funds. The labor market is the other side of the ledger. June payrolls came in at 158,984 — fine — and the unemployment rate beat expectations at 4.2%, but the story under the surface is less clean. Hires are down 3.0% YoY, the quits rate sits at 1.9% — a level historically associated with workers who feel stuck, not mobile — and the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) rose 17.3% YoY to 1.94 million, even as the headline ticked down in June. That cohort matters less for July listings appointments than it does for delinquency pipelines 12 to 18 months out. Regionally, the Case-Shiller April data (the most recent available, released early July) shows the geographic split sharpening: Chicago is up 6.4% YoY and New York up 3.7%, while Dallas is -1.7%, Denver -1.8%, Phoenix -1.8%, and Seattle -2.2% — all running negative for nearly a year.
