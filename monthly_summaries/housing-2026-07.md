# Monthly Housing Summary — July 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held positive on a four-week average basis — the Altos 4-week average came in at +4.4% YoY as of June 26 — but the spot reading flipped negative at -3.0% YoY, the first negative week after a 55-consecutive-week streak of positive YoY pending sales. That streak ending deserves attention, though one week doesn't rewrite the trend: the 13-week average remains at +4.8% YoY and has risen for 14 consecutive weeks, suggesting the underlying demand picture is softer but not broken. Pending days on market ticked up to 70.9 days, now +6.7% YoY for the 103rd consecutive week — homes are taking longer to go under contract than a year ago, even in a low-inventory environment. Altos total inventory reached 1.07 million, up 1.2% YoY, with new listings decelerating: the spot reading fell -7.1% YoY and the 4-week average is barely positive at +0.4% YoY, consistent with sellers pulling back as the summer selling season peaks. NAR existing home sales for May printed at 4.17 million SAAR, up 3.2% YoY and well ahead of the 3.85 million consensus — the cleanest beat of the year so far. On an NSA basis the YoY change was flat, which is the honest read; the SAAR beat reflects favorable seasonal adjustment. New home sales told a different story: May came in at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY, with months supply rising to 10.3 — builders are sitting on elevated finished inventory as starts fell sharply (-8.2% YoY, missing the 1,350,000 consensus by nearly 175,000 units). Single-family completions fell to their lowest YoY reading since June 2011, down 17.4%. The divergence between existing and new home sales is notable: resale demand is holding, but buyers appear to be choosing existing homes over new construction at the margin, likely because builder concessions are not fully offsetting the price gap. The 30-year fixed rate stands at 6.68% today, having pulled back from recent highs before ticking up two basis points this week as the 10-year Treasury rose to 4.59%. The 30Y-10Y spread sits at 2.09 percentage points — still elevated relative to the historical norm of around 1.7 points, but down 30 basis points YoY, meaning the compression story is quietly playing out. If the spread continues toward 1.85-1.90, the 30-year rate falls meaningfully without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications were -6.3% YoY on the most recent week, though the 4-week average held at +0.5% YoY before slipping; the 13-week average remains +5.1% YoY. More important: the Xactus MII, which leads MBA by 2-4 weeks, bounced off recent lows with the 4-week average turning up after several weeks of decline. That divergence — MII recovering while MBA weakens — suggests the MBA softness may be holiday-week noise rather than a genuine demand break. Watch whether MII holds its recovery through mid-July; if it does, the MBA applications data should stabilize by late July.

The macro picture this month is genuinely mixed in ways that matter for housing. June payrolls came in at approximately 159,000 — the exact consensus figure is harder to pin down from available data, but the unemployment rate fell to 4.2% against a 4.4% consensus expectation, the most decisive labor beat of the quarter. A lower-than-expected unemployment rate keeps the household formation engine running and reduces the probability of a near-term demand shock from job losses. That said, long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) stands at 1.937 million, up 17.3% YoY — this cohort does not affect the current selling season, but it is accumulating at a pace that historically precedes a rise in mortgage delinquencies 6-12 months out. The quits rate held at 1.9%, down 0.2 points YoY, and the hires rate was flat; neither signals labor market stress, but neither signals the job-hopping confidence that tends to unlock move-up purchases. On inflation, Core PCE for May printed at +3.41% YoY — above the Fed's 2% target and above the headline CPI trend, which means the Fed remains on hold longer than rate futures were pricing earlier in the year.
