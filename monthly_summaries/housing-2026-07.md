The Altos 13-week pending average has now posted positive year-over-year readings for 55 consecutive weeks, and the 4-week average sits at +4.4% YoY — though the most recent single-week print (week ending June 26) dipped to -3.0% YoY, snapping a positive streak. That single-week softness deserves attention but not alarm: the 13-week trend is still rising, and pending days on market at 70.9 days are running about 6.7% above last year, meaning contracts are taking longer to form, not evaporating. Inventory on the Altos feed reached 1.07 million active listings, up 1.2% YoY — growth that has held but is no longer accelerating. New listings told a more nuanced story: the single-week reading fell 7.1% YoY (reversing several weeks of positive prints), while the 4-week average remains barely positive at +0.4% and the 13-week average has been rising for 14 consecutive weeks at +0.7% YoY. The inventory picture is one of a slow seasonal build that has stalled near its prior-year level, not a flood. NAR's June existing sales came in at 4.09 million SAAR — well ahead of the 3.85 million consensus — and are up 4.1% YoY on the seasonally adjusted basis, with the NSA count up 7.9% YoY; that acceleration is real. The contrast with new home sales is worth noting: the May new-home SAAR of 580,000 is down 7.5% YoY with months supply at 10.3, confirming that builders are still working through an elevated pipeline rather than generating fresh demand. Single-family starts fell 7.1% YoY in May and single-family completions are down 17.4% YoY — the weakest pace since June 2011 — so the resale market will not be bailed out by new supply anytime soon. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.65% as of July 9, down a few basis points on the week, but the mortgage-Treasury spread — the gap between the 30-year rate and the 10-year Treasury yield, historically around 1.7 percentage points — remains at 2.10-2.13pp. That excess spread of roughly 40 basis points above the historical norm is the hidden affordability lever: if credit markets normalize even partially, the 30-year rate could ease to the low-to-mid 6s without any Fed action. The leading indicators are sending mixed signals worth watching closely. The MBA purchase application 4-week average is +0.5% YoY but the most recent week turned -6.3% YoY — the first negative print in 73 weeks on the 13-week trend basis. More telling: the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (a credit-inquiry-based measure that tends to lead MBA applications by 2-4 weeks) saw its 4-week average tick up in late June after several weeks of decline, suggesting the MBA weakness may be transient noise around the July 4 holiday week rather than a trend break. Over the next four weeks, watch whether the Altos single-week pending number recovers its positive YoY footing and whether the MBA 4-week average holds above zero — those two readings will tell you whether June's strong NAR print was the start of a firmer second half or a seasonal anomaly.

The macro backdrop is holding better than the consensus expected, though the details carry a housing-relevant caveat. June payrolls printed at roughly 159,000 — above the 59,000 Dow Jones consensus — and the unemployment rate fell 10 basis points to 4.2%, also beating expectations of 4.4%. Wage growth at 3.5% YoY means real household purchasing power is positive, which supports mortgage qualification. The more important signal for housing, however, is the long-term unemployed cohort: workers unemployed 27 weeks or longer reached 1.937 million in June, up 17.3% YoY. That cohort does not disqualify buyers today — most of them are not in the purchase pipeline — but it is the leading edge of the distressed-borrower story that will show up in delinquency data 6-12 months from now, particularly in Sun Belt markets where price appreciation has already turned negative. On inflation, May core PCE came in at 3.4% YoY — above the Fed's 2% target and above what the bond market needs to price in rate cuts with confidence. The 10-year Treasury is holding at 4.55%, and with the spread still elevated, the rate relief agents have been waiting for is gated more by the spread normalizing than by the Fed moving. Shelter CPI at 3.35% YoY is still running hot, but it is a lagged measure of rents that peaked in the real-time data roughly 18 months ago; it will mechanically slow through the back half of 2026 and pull core PCE lower with it.
