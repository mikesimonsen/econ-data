# Monthly Housing Summary — July 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales held at +4.81% YoY for the 55th consecutive week, which is the signal to anchor on even as the most recent weekly read ticked to -2.99% YoY — a normal seasonal softening around the holiday week that the four-week average, running at +4.37% YoY, largely absorbs. Demand is positive on a trend basis, but it is decelerating: the four-week average has pulled back from the mid-single-digit YoY gains of earlier in the spring, and pending days on market at 70.9 days are running 6.7% above last year, meaning homes are sitting longer before going under contract. Altos inventory reached 1.07 million active listings, up 1.2% YoY — growth that has been remarkably stable but is now clearly decelerating from the double-digit YoY gains of 2024 and early 2025. New listings tell a more nuanced story: the single-week read fell to -7.1% YoY, but the four-week average held barely positive at +0.41% and the 13-week average has been rising for 14 consecutive weeks at +0.71% YoY, so supply is not collapsing — sellers are just exhibiting typical early-July hesitation. NAR existing home sales for May printed at 4.17 million SAAR, beating the 3.85 million consensus by a meaningful margin and rising 3.2% YoY, with the Midwest and South driving the acceleration. That diverges sharply from new home sales, which fell to 580,000 SAAR in May, down 7.5% YoY, with months of supply for new homes expanding to 10.3 — builders are carrying elevated unsold inventory even as existing-home supply remains lean. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.63% as of July 7, ticking back up four basis points after a brief softening run. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.14 percentage points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points — meaning a meaningful affordability improvement is available without any Fed action if credit markets normalize. That spread has compressed 25-32 basis points over the past year, and if it continues toward historical norms, the 30-year could fall into the low-to-mid 6% range even with the 10-year anchored near 4.5%. MBA purchase applications for the week ending July 3 came in at a four-week average of +0.5% YoY — decelerating sharply from the +5% YoY on the 13-week average — and the most recent week went negative at -6.3% YoY, which warrants attention but carries holiday noise. The Xactus MII, which leads MBA by two to four weeks, reversed off recent lows and ticked up in its four-week average (+2.3%), suggesting the MBA softness may be transitory rather than a trend break. Watch whether the Xactus MII holds that recovery over the next two weeks — if it does, purchase applications should stabilize in late July. Builder starts fell sharply in May to 1,177,000 SAAR, missing expectations by 13%, with single-family starts down 7.1% YoY; but single-family permits edged up 1.25% in May and are holding near 890,000, so builders are completing their backlog faster than they are authorizing new supply — a pipeline that implies tighter resale competition from new homes in 2026 but less so in 2027.

The labor market continues to provide a floor under housing demand without adding much acceleration. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — modest but sufficient to sustain household formation — and the unemployment rate fell to 4.2%, beating the 4.4% consensus. The mechanism is straightforward: employed households with stable incomes can carry a mortgage at 6.63%; what keeps them out of the market is affordability, not job security. The more cautionary signal is the long-term unemployed cohort, which rose 17.3% YoY in June to 1.94 million even as the headline rate improved — that cohort, people out of work 27 weeks or more, exhausts savings and eventually misses mortgage payments. It does not affect this selling season, but watch it accumulate: it is the leading edge of future delinquency pressure, particularly in markets where home equity cushions are thin. On inflation, core PCE ran at 3.41% YoY in May, and core CPI printed at 2.82% YoY — both came in cooler than consensus, which is why the 10-year Treasury has held near 4.49% rather than repricing higher. The shelter CPI component ran at 3.35% YoY in May, still elevated but continuing to drift down as the lagged effects of 2023–2024 rent normalization flow through the index; when shelter CPI falls toward 2.5%, the Fed's path to its 2% target becomes considerably cleaner.
