**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive in the aggregate even as the spot read softened. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales stands at roughly 89,700 units — up 4.8% year-over-year and positive for 55 consecutive weeks — which is the signal that matters most through the seasonal noise. The 4-week average printed at 89,200, up 4.4% YoY, though decelerating from the pace we saw in May. The spot week ending June 26 dipped 3.0% YoY as pending sales fell for a third consecutive week, so watch whether that softness carries into July data. Inventory reached 1.07 million active listings, up 1.2% YoY, still well below any historically normal level of supply. New listings have lost momentum: the spot week fell 7.1% YoY and the 4-week average is barely clinging to flat (+0.4% YoY), with the 13-week trend still rising but decelerating. Pending days on market at 70.9 days is up 6.7% YoY — a real number, consistent with buyers taking more time — though it has not blown out enough to indicate a stall. NAR's June existing home sales printed at 4.09 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a wide margin; YoY that is +4.1% and accelerating, keeping a 36-month positive price streak intact at a $440,600 national median. On an NSA basis — the cleaner comparison this time of year — June sales ran +7.9% YoY. New home sales diverged sharply: May printed at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY, with months of supply climbing to 10.3 — builders are sitting on inventory while existing-home buyers transact. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.75% today, essentially flat year-over-year and up just a few basis points over the past month. The more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: at 2.12 percentage points versus a historical norm of roughly 1.7, there remains 40 basis points of compression available before the 10-year even has to move. That spread has now narrowed 28 basis points YoY — the hidden affordability story most clients haven't heard. MBA purchase applications (4-week average through July 10) flipped to -0.6% YoY, the first negative reading after a 74-week positive streak on the 13-week average, which warrants attention. The Xactus MII — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks by tracking early credit-pull activity — turned up on both the spot and 4-week basis after declining, suggesting the MBA softness may be transient rather than a reversal. Watch the next two Xactus prints: if MII firms through mid-July, the MBA weakness likely reflects holiday-week distortion. June housing starts beat at 1,427,000 SAAR, but the composition matters — single-family starts fell a third consecutive month to 895,000 while multifamily drove the headline. Permits told the opposite story: total permits missed at 1,367,000, down 1.9% YoY, with the South specifically dropping 8.8% YoY. Builders are delivering existing pipeline but pulling back on future single-family authorization, which has implications for 2027 supply more than for this selling season.

The macro backdrop shifted in a meaningful way this month, and the direction is constructive for housing. June CPI came in far cooler than expected — headline fell 0.4% month-over-month against a consensus call of +0.9%, and core printed essentially flat — pushing core CPI to 2.57% YoY, the lowest read in this cycle. That matters for housing through two channels: it anchors the 10-year Treasury (currently 4.63%, up modestly on the day but well below where it would be if inflation re-accelerated), and it gives the Fed room to cut without abandoning its credibility, even though core PCE for May still ran at 3.41% YoY and shelter CPI continues to print a steady 3.3% YoY — slower than the prior year but not yet fully cleared. The labor market gave agents a cleaner story than feared: June payrolls came in at 159,000, unemployment fell to 4.2% against a consensus of 4.4%, and initial claims for the week of July 11 dropped to 208,000, the lowest in months. The quits rate at 1.9% and the hires rate at 3.3% are both running below year-ago levels, which means labor market mobility — the mechanism by which workers relocate and generate housing transactions — remains subdued even if layoffs are not rising. The one number worth watching carefully is the long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks): at 1.94 million it is up 17% YoY even as it ticked down slightly in June.
