**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held firmer than the headline week suggests. The Altos new pending sales spot reading for the week of June 26 slipped 3.0% year-over-year — the first negative YoY week after what had been a sustained run of positive reads — but that single-week dip reflects normal holiday-week noise. The 4-week average remains +4.4% YoY, and the 13-week average sits at +4.8% YoY, now positive for 55 consecutive weeks. That streak is the more honest read on underlying demand, and it continues to hold. Inventory rose for the eighth consecutive week, reaching roughly 1.07 million active listings (Altos, SFR + condo), up about 1.2% YoY — growth that has decelerated considerably from the 20-30% YoY clips of early 2025. New listings are also moderating: the 4-week average is just +0.4% YoY, and the most recent week turned negative (-7.1% YoY), though the 13-week trend is still slightly positive. Pending days on market have risen to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — the market is absorbing supply more slowly than a year ago, but not seizing up. The May NAR existing home sales print came in at 4.17 million SAAR, beating the 3.85 million consensus by a wide margin and running +3.2% YoY — the second consecutive monthly gain. The NSA YoY was flat, which is the honest comparison for that series. New home sales told the opposite story: 580,000 SAAR in May, down 7.5% YoY, with months supply at 10.3 — builders are sitting on finished inventory while starts fell sharply (-8.2% YoY, -15.5% month-over-month), missing the 1,350,000 consensus by a significant margin. The divergence between existing and new is worth watching: builders are not pulling back on permits at the same rate (single-family permits -0.8% YoY, holding near flat), but starts are contracting, which implies they are completing the pipeline rather than adding to it. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.59% as of today, down 20 basis points YoY. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.11 points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.70 points. That excess spread is the affordability lever most buyers are not aware of: if credit and volatility conditions normalize and the spread compresses even 20-25 basis points, the 30-year could fall toward 6.3% without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications (4-week average, +3.1% YoY) confirm the demand signal. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by 2-4 weeks — ticked up on its 4-week average after seven weeks of declines, with its YoY trend improving; that recovery is tentative but worth noting as a potential early read on July application volume. Watch whether the MII holds above its recent trough through mid-July before drawing conclusions.

The macro backdrop is more supportive than the consensus narrative allows. June payrolls came in at 159,000 jobs (well above the 59,000 Dow Jones consensus), and the unemployment rate fell to 4.2% against a 4.4% expectation — both readings indicate the labor market softened less than feared. Average hourly earnings rose 3.5% YoY, which keeps real wage growth modestly positive and supports buyer purchasing power. The mechanism for housing is direct: employed workers with rising real wages are more likely to pull the trigger on a purchase; the question is whether confidence follows. Consumer confidence at 91.2 beat expectations but remains below year-ago levels, and that hesitancy is showing up in the quits rate (1.9%, down 0.2 points YoY) — people are less willing to voluntarily leave jobs, which suppresses the job-change-driven relocations that generate housing transactions. The long-term unemployed cohort (those out of work 27+ weeks) ticked down in June to 1.937 million but remains 17% above year-ago levels; this does not affect the current selling season, but it is the variable to watch for any future uptick in mortgage delinquencies 9-12 months out. On inflation, Core PCE ran 3.41% YoY in May — still above the Fed's 2% target — while shelter CPI held at 3.35% YoY. Core PCE above 3% makes it difficult for the Fed to cut, which keeps the 10-year Treasury anchored near 4.48% and limits how far the 30-year can fall through Fed action alone; spread compression remains the more plausible near-term rate relief story. Regionally, the Case-Shiller data through March/April (remember the roughly two-month lag) continues to show Chicago up nearly 6% YoY, New York up 4%, Cleveland up 3%, and Boston up 2% — while Dallas is -1.7%, Phoenix -1.8%, Denver -1.
