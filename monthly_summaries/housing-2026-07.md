**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through the four weeks ending June 26, though the headline reads require some disaggregation. The Altos new pending sales 13-week average sits at +4.81% year over year — 55 consecutive weeks of positive YoY readings — while the 4-week average, at +4.37%, has decelerated from its recent peak. The spot weekly print for June 26 flipped negative (-2.99% YoY) for the first time in that streak, which is worth watching but not yet a signal: holiday-week distortions routinely produce one-week breaks before mean-reverting. Inventory continued to build, with Altos total supply at roughly 1.075 million units, up about 1.2% YoY over eight consecutive rising weeks. More notable is what new listings are doing: the spot week turned negative (-7.1% YoY) after several weeks in positive territory, and the 4-week average is barely holding at +0.4% YoY. The 13-week average remains constructive at +0.7% YoY, but the deceleration implies the inventory expansion is running more on reduced absorption than on fresh supply — a subtle but important distinction for pricing. Pending days on market sit at 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, which confirms the modest softening in transaction velocity. NAR existing home sales for June came in at a 4.09 million SAAR, down roughly 1.9% from the revised May figure but up 4.1% YoY — well ahead of the 3.85 million consensus — and the NSA count at 422,000 is up 7.9% from June 2025. New home sales told a different story: the May print at 580,000 SAAR fell 7.4% from April and sits 7.5% below year-ago levels, with months supply at 10.3 — elevated territory that explains why single-family starts dropped to 882,000 and total starts came in at 1,177,000, roughly 15% below the prior month and well short of the 1.35 million consensus. Single-family permits ticked up to 892,000 but remain negative YoY for the 24th consecutive month, and with the pipeline under construction contracting for the 42nd straight month on the single-family side, the production shortfall is durable — it will show up in resale competition well into 2027. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.64% as of July 10, essentially unchanged over the past month. The more meaningful number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: at approximately 2.11 points on a 4-week average basis, it remains well above the long-run norm of roughly 1.7 points. That 40-basis-point excess is the hidden affordability lever — if credit conditions normalize even partially, the 30-year could fall to the low-6s without any Fed movement. MBA purchase applications for the week ending July 3 dipped to -6.3% YoY on the spot read, but the 4-week average remains slightly positive at +0.5%; the 13-week average at +5.1% YoY is the anchor. More instructive is the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — a credit-pull based measure that leads MBA by roughly 2-4 weeks — which bounced to +3.5% week over week after declining for several weeks, though it remains -5.6% YoY on the spot and -2.3% on the 4-week average. That MII-MBA divergence is the most actionable signal right now: the MII's recent uptick suggests the July 4 week softness in MBA applications may not persist, but the sustained YoY negative on MII counsels against assuming a resumption of the spring momentum. Watch the next two MII readings closely before concluding the deceleration in pending sales has reversed.

The macro backdrop in June was better than expected on nearly every dimension that directly feeds mortgage demand, and that matters more than it sounds. June payrolls printed at roughly 159,000 — a solid number — and the unemployment rate fell a tenth to 4.2%, beating the 4.4% consensus. Wage growth held at 3.5% YoY, which keeps real income positive against a shelter CPI running at 3.4% annually. The mechanism for housing is straightforward: employed, modestly wage-growing households are the pool of qualified buyers; a 4.2% unemployment rate does not produce the demand destruction that drives price corrections. The one labor market signal worth tracking is the long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — which ticked down in June to 1.937 million but remains 17.3% above a year ago. That cohort does not threaten the current selling season, but it is the future distressed-borrower pipeline; watch it accumulate over the second half. On inflation, core PCE for May came in at 3.4% YoY and core CPI at 2.8%, both cooler than consensus, which reinforces the case that the Fed's next move is more likely a cut than a hike — and that matters for the Treasury
