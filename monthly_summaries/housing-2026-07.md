**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive in aggregate but showed clear deceleration heading into July. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales sits at +4.81% year-over-year and has been in positive territory for 55 consecutive weeks — a durable trend — but the most recent weekly read flipped negative at -2.99% YoY, and the 4-week average decelerated to +4.37% from levels above +6% earlier in the quarter. That single-week negative print deserves attention rather than alarm: summer holiday weeks routinely distort the weekly series, and the 13-week trend has not rolled over. Inventory rose for the eighth consecutive week to roughly 1.07 million units on Altos (+1.2% YoY), a healthy but unremarkable build. New listings told a more complicated story: the 4-week average held barely positive at +0.41% YoY, the 13-week average has been rising for 14 weeks, but the most recent weekly print turned negative at -7.1% YoY — suggesting sellers pulled back around the Fourth of July holiday rather than a structural retreat. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, +6.7% YoY, a modest but persistent signal that absorption is a little slower than a year ago. NAR existing sales for June came in at 4.09 million SAAR, beating consensus by a wide margin (expected: 3.85M) and running +4.1% YoY on the headline, though the month-over-month SAAR edged down slightly; the NSA figure of 422,000 was up +7.9% YoY, which is the more reliable directional read here. New home sales diverged meaningfully — 628,000 SAAR, still down 5.1% YoY — and new home median prices fell to $398,300 (-2.7% YoY), with months supply at 9.3, well above the existing-home supply of 4.6 months. Builders are clearing pipeline, not building conviction: single-family starts fell for the third consecutive month even as the total starts headline jumped on a multifamily pop; single-family permits fell to 872,000 — positive YoY for the first time since May 2024, but ticking down month-over-month. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.81% today, essentially flat year-over-year, but the 30Y–10Y spread remains at 2.13–2.14 percentage points against a historical norm of roughly 1.7pp. That spread has compressed 27 basis points YoY, and if it moves another 20–25bp toward historical norms without the 10-year cooperating, the conforming rate could fall into the mid-6s without any Fed action — the affordability story most buyers aren't being told. MBA purchase applications rose +5.5% in the most recent week, flipping back to a bare +0.4% YoY after several negative weeks, but the 4-week average remains -1.2% YoY. The Xactus MII — which leads MBA by roughly 2–4 weeks — ticked up to 123.3 on the most recent read after a decline, and its 4-week average also reversed upward, but the 13-week average has been falling for seven weeks and sits -2.4% YoY. That divergence between the short-term MII bounce and the still-declining 13-week trend is the leading indicator to track through August — it will tell us whether the MBA applications recovery has legs.

On the macro side, three data points matter most for housing right now. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — solidly above the 59,000 consensus — and unemployment fell to 4.2% against a 4.4% expectation, which removes the near-term recession narrative that was beginning to build in rate markets. A labor market that continues adding jobs at this pace keeps household formation intact and supports demand at the margin. The more consequential read was June CPI: headline fell -0.4% month-over-month against an expected +0.9%, pushing the YoY to +3.46%; core CPI came in nearly flat at -0.02% MoM versus a +0.3% expectation, bringing core to +2.57% YoY. Shelter CPI continued to rise (+0.12% MoM, +3.26% YoY) — still running above the Fed's target, which means the central bank's path to cuts remains constrained by the very component that housing feeds. Core PCE for May held at +3.41% YoY, above the 2% target, so the Fed is not moving imminently. The mechanism: if core PCE and core CPI continue to soften in the July prints, rate-cut expectations will pull forward in the futures market, which would put downward pressure on the 10-year and, with spread compression doing additional work, could meaningfully lower the 30-year.
