**July 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held firmer than the headline numbers suggest entering July. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks and sits at +4.81% year-over-year, still rising — the streak and the trend matter more than the spot week, which dipped to -2.99% YoY in the final week of June, likely noise amplified by the holiday-shortened period. The 4-week average at +4.37% YoY tells the cleaner story. Total active inventory reached 1.07 million units nationally, up about 1.2% YoY — growth that is clearly decelerating from the double-digit YoY expansions of 2024-2025, and the Altos new listings 4-week average ticked only +0.41% YoY after several weeks positive, suggesting the supply pipeline is tightening again. Pending days on market extended to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, which is real — buyers are taking longer to commit — but the sale-to-list ratio at 0.99 and the share of homes going off market within two weeks at a post-February-2024 high tell you the homes that do transact are trading cleanly. NAR existing home sales for May printed at 4.17 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a meaningful margin and accelerating to +3.2% YoY; the NSA count was flat year-over-year, consistent with a genuine but not dramatic improvement in activity. New home sales diverged sharply: May came in at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY and falling for a second consecutive month, with months supply at 10.3 — builders are sitting on finished inventory while existing-home supply tightens around them. Single-family starts fell to 882,000 SAAR in May, down 7.1% YoY, and single-family permits at 892,000 have been negative YoY for 24 consecutive months, so the production pipeline is not rebuilding. The 30-year fixed rate eased five basis points to 6.60% as of July 2, a modest reversal after weeks of pressure. The spread to the 10-year Treasury ticked down to 2.13 points — still roughly 45 basis points above the long-run norm of ~1.70 — but the spread has compressed 30 basis points year-over-year. That compression is the affordability story the headlines are missing: if it narrows another 25-30 basis points toward historical norms, the 30-year rate falls meaningfully without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications ticked up to +3.1% YoY on the 4-week average. The Xactus MII divergence is worth watching: the 4-week average reversed higher (+2.3% move) after several weeks of softening, but the 13-week average has been falling for seven weeks and sits at -2.4% YoY — the MII typically leads MBA by 2-4 weeks, and the persistent 13-week downtrend warns that the MBA rebound may be fragile. Over the next four weeks, watch whether the MII 4-week average sustains its reversal or rolls back over; that will be the first read on August purchase demand.

The macro picture improved modestly but unevenly. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — the number itself was unremarkable — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.2% against a consensus expectation of 4.4%, a genuine beat. For housing, the mechanism is direct: lower unemployment supports both buyer confidence and mortgage qualification. The counterweight is the long-term unemployed cohort, which at 1.937 million remains 17% above year-ago levels even after ticking down in June; this cohort does not disrupt the current selling season, but it will shape distressed-borrower volume 6-12 months out and deserves a watch on your radar. Inflation is the more complex signal. Headline CPI came in at +4.2% YoY in May — well above the Fed's target — but core CPI printed +2.8% YoY, cooler than expected, and core PCE at +3.4% is the number the Fed actually targets. Shelter CPI at +3.35% YoY is still sticky but decelerating, and because shelter CPI lags private-market rent data by roughly 12-18 months, the further deceleration in Zillow's ZORI (now +2.95% YoY) implies shelter CPI will keep pulling core inflation lower into late 2026. That is the disinflationary path that could allow the Fed to move — but the 10-year Treasury rose 11 basis points to 4.48% in the most recent week, partly reversing June's relief, which is why spread compression rather than rate-level movement is the rate story to track.
