# Monthly Housing Summary — July 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales remained in positive territory on a year-over-year basis through the week of June 26, with the Altos 13-week average sitting at roughly +4.8% YoY after 55 consecutive weeks above the prior-year line — a streak that has now run more than a year. The four-week average held at +4.4% YoY, though the most recent weekly read tipped negative (-3.0% YoY), which is worth watching but is not yet a trend break; single-week reads in late June routinely soften against strong year-ago comps. Inventory continued to build, with Altos total active supply up about 1.2% YoY at 1.075 million units as of June 26, rising for eight consecutive weeks, while the Realtor.com active count moved to 1.10 million, up 1.9% YoY. New listings, however, decelerated sharply: the spot Altos weekly new-listing count fell 7.1% YoY and the four-week average was barely positive at +0.4% YoY, suggesting the inventory expansion is increasingly driven by homes sitting longer rather than fresh supply entering the market — a distinction that matters for how you price. Pending days on market at 70.9 days ran 6.7% above year-ago levels, consistent with that pattern. NAR's June existing-home sales print came in at 4.09 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a meaningful margin, and running +4.1% YoY on a seasonally adjusted basis and +7.9% YoY on the raw NSA count — the strongest year-over-year comparison in several years. That said, the SAAR figure ticked down from May, so the beat reflects a low consensus bar rather than an acceleration. New home sales diverged considerably: the May read came in at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY, and new-home months supply pushed to 10.3 — a level that signals builders are working through a backlog rather than chasing new demand. The 30-year fixed rate stands at 6.85% as of July 23, up about 8 basis points on the week and modestly elevated relative to a month ago. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.14 percentage points — still well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points, which means if credit and volatility conditions normalize, the 30-year could fall toward 6.2–6.3% without any Fed move at all. That spread compression is the affordability lever most buyers and agents are not pricing in. On leading indicators, the MBA purchase index for the week of July 17 bounced +5.5% week-over-week and turned just barely positive YoY (+0.4%), but the four-week average remains -1.2% YoY and has fallen for three consecutive weeks — the bounce does not yet change the trend. More notable: the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (a credit-inquiry-based measure that tends to lead MBA purchase applications by two to four weeks) turned higher in late June after a prolonged decline, with the four-week MII average reversing upward. If that signal holds into early July data, it would imply MBA purchase volume should firm in the next reading or two. Watch for the next MII print to confirm or deny that reversal; if it stalls, the MBA bounce may be noise.

The macro backdrop shifted meaningfully in July. June payrolls came in at approximately 159,000, above the Dow Jones consensus of 59,000 (the consensus appeared to embed significant tariff-disruption pessimism), and the unemployment rate fell to 4.2% against expectations of 4.4% — the kind of labor market resilience that keeps prospective sellers employed and in their homes, which suppresses voluntary listing activity, but also keeps buyers qualified. The more consequential number was June CPI: headline came in at -0.4% month-over-month against an expected +0.9%, and core CPI was essentially flat at -0.02% MoM versus an expected +0.3%, bringing core YoY to 2.57%. That is a sharp deceleration, and if it prints similarly in July, it reopens the conversation about Fed rate cuts in September. Core PCE for May was still running at 3.4% YoY, so the Fed has cover to wait, but the gap between CPI and PCE on core will be worth watching — PCE's June print (due late July) is the single most important macro data point for housing rates in the next four weeks. Shelter CPI continued to edge up, +0.12% MoM and +3.26% YoY, which will keep core PCE from collapsing even if goods prices stay soft. On long-term labor market risk: the pool of workers unemployed 27 weeks or longer reached 1.937 million, up 17.3% YoY. That cohort does not threaten the current selling season, but it is the delinquency pipeline for 2027 and bears watching as it accumulates.
