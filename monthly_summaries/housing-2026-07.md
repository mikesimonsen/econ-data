# Monthly Housing Summary — July 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held positive year-over-year for the 55th consecutive week on the Altos 13-week average, which sits at +4.81% YoY — the most reliable read we have on underlying demand through the seasonal noise. The spot week (ending June 26) flipped to -2.99% YoY and has fallen three consecutive weeks, but the four-week average remains a healthier +4.37% YoY, so the spot softness looks more like a seasonal timing effect than a demand reversal. Inventory continued its patient climb, with Altos total active supply up about 1.2% YoY at roughly 1.07 million units — still growing, but the growth rate is decelerating relative to the spring pace, and new listings ticked negative YoY (-7.1% on the spot week) for the first time after several months of modest gains; the four-week average held barely positive at +0.4% YoY, suggesting the pullback may be brief. Pending days on market are running about 7% above last year at 70.9 days, meaning homes are taking longer to go under contract, which is consistent with buyers having slightly more negotiating room in many markets. NAR's June existing sales print came in at 4.09 million SAAR — a meaningful beat versus the 3.85 million consensus, and up 4.1% YoY on the SAAR basis; on the NSA count it's +7.9% YoY, a cleaner comparison given the calendar. That said, the SAAR figure ticked down month-over-month, ending a short rise, while new home sales (May print) fell to 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY with builder supply at 10.3 months — a level that historically prompts incentive-driven price concessions rather than production cuts. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.68% as of July 16, reversing a modest recent decline, with the spread to the 10-year Treasury at roughly 2.13% on a four-week average — still about 40 basis points above the long-run historical norm of ~1.7%. That excess spread is the understated affordability lever: if credit and volatility conditions normalize even partially, the 30-year can fall meaningfully without any Fed action. On leading indicators, the MBA purchase index four-week average turned negative YoY (-0.6%) for the first time in over a year, which warrants attention — but the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks, capturing consumer rate-shopping behavior before applications are formally submitted) reversed higher off recent lows, with its four-week average up 2.3% and its YoY trend improving. That divergence is worth tracking: if the MII follow-through is real, MBA applications should firm in late July or early August. Starts fell sharply in May (-15.5% from April, -8.2% YoY), missing expectations by a wide margin, while single-family permits held roughly flat at 892,000 SAAR — still negative YoY for the 24th consecutive month, confirming builders are not adding meaningfully to the future pipeline. The construction picture says less supply is coming in 2027, which tightens the medium-term inventory outlook even as today's active counts edge higher.

The macro backdrop shifted notably in the past four weeks. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 — above a 59,000 consensus expectation that had absorbed significant tariff-related anxiety — and the unemployment rate fell to 4.2% against a 4.4% consensus, suggesting the labor market is absorbing trade policy disruption better than many anticipated. That matters for housing directly: employed households buy homes; a labor market that holds together sustains purchase demand even when affordability is strained. The long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) did tick lower month-over-month to 1.937 million, a welcome reversal, but it remains 17% above a year ago — that accumulation is not a current-selling-season story, but it is the future delinquency story, worth tracking for the distressed-listing pipeline six to twelve months out. On inflation, June CPI came in well below expectations — headline fell 0.4% month-over-month against an expected +0.9%, and core was essentially flat against a +0.3% forecast. Shelter CPI continued its slow deceleration, rising just 3.3% YoY, consistent with the real-time rent data (Zillow's ZORI is at +3.3% YoY, a three-year high but still reflecting new-lease pricing, not the lagged CPI basket). Core PCE for May came in at +3.4% YoY — still above the Fed's 2% target — so the softer CPI print doesn't immediately translate to rate cuts, but it does compress inflation expectations (breakevens, the market-implied inflation rate embedded in TIPS pricing, have likely eased), which historically allows the 10-year Treasury to drift lower and pulls mortgage rates with it.
