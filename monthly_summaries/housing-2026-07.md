# Monthly Housing Summary — July 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

Demand held positive over the past four weeks, though the signal requires some care in reading. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales stands at +4.81% year-over-year and has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks — a streak that still argues for underlying market health. The 4-week average sits at +4.37% YoY. The spot week ending June 26 dipped to -2.99% YoY, breaking a run of positive weeks, and pending sales have fallen three consecutive weeks — typical late-June seasonality, but worth watching to confirm it doesn't extend. Inventory at 1.07 million active listings is up 1.17% YoY, a modest pace relative to the prior year's growth rate; the 13-week average for new listings is up only 0.71% YoY after the spot week turned negative at -7.14%, suggesting the fresh-supply pipeline is decelerating into summer. Pending days on market at 70.9 days are running about 6.7% above last year — homes are taking longer to go under contract, a slow-motion shift more than an acute one. NAR's June existing home sales print was 4.09 million SAAR, +4.1% YoY and well above the 3.85 million consensus, though the NSA read of 422,000 is the cleaner directional signal: up 7.9% YoY. Contrast that with new home sales through May at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY with months supply at 10.3 — builders are sitting on finished inventory and the market is absorbing it slowly. The 30-year fixed rate ticked back to 6.71% as of July 20, reversing a modest decline, with the 10-year Treasury at 4.63%. The 30Y-10Y spread sits at 2.15 percentage points — still roughly 45 basis points above the long-run norm of about 1.70pp. That gap represents the hidden affordability story: if the spread compresses back toward historical norms without any Fed move, rates fall meaningfully without a policy change. The MBA purchase index 4-week average is -0.60% YoY as of July 10 and just turned negative after a run of positive readings — a caution flag. The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks (it measures mortgage credit inquiries before applications are filed), stands at -5.59% YoY on the spot reading and -2.30% on the 4-week average. The MII's 13-week average has been falling for seven straight weeks. When MII leads MBA lower, as it appears to be doing now, purchase application softness tends to follow within a month. Watch whether the MBA 4-week average deteriorates further in the next two readings — that is the clearest near-term demand signal.

The macro backdrop is genuinely mixed in ways that matter for housing. June payrolls came in at roughly 159,000 with the unemployment rate falling to 4.2%, beating a 4.4% consensus — labor market still expanding, not contracting. Average hourly earnings rose 3.5% YoY, which keeps real income positive and supports buyer capacity. The more important labor data point, however, is the long-term unemployed cohort: those out of work 27 weeks or longer stand at 1.937 million, up 17.3% YoY. That cohort does not affect the current selling season, but it is the leading indicator for mortgage delinquency stress 6-12 months out; watch it accumulate. On inflation, June CPI came in far cooler than expected — headline fell 0.4% month-over-month when consensus expected a 0.9% gain, and core came in flat versus a 0.3% forecast. Shelter CPI rose just 0.12% in June and is up 3.26% YoY, continuing its deceleration from the 2023 peak; that deceleration will feed into lower core inflation readings over coming months, which is the mechanism by which the Fed eventually gets room to move. Core PCE through May is still at 3.41% YoY — above target — so the Fed's hands remain tied for now. The Case-Shiller April data (which reflects contracts signed in February and March) tells the two-speed story clearly: Chicago is up 6.4% YoY and New York up 3.7%, while Dallas is -1.7%, Phoenix -1.8%, Denver -1.8%, and Seattle -2.2%. Agents in Midwest and Northeast metros are working a different price conversation than agents in Sun Belt markets, and that divergence is widening, not closing. The one signal to watch for next month: whether the cooler-than-expected June CPI reading triggers a meaningful compression in the 30Y-10Y spread. If Treasury yields soften on revised Fed-cut expectations and the spread holds or narrows, the 30-year rate could pull back from 6.71% without any Fed action — and that is what would actually move buyer behavior in August.
