# Monthly Housing Summary — June 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held positive for the 53rd consecutive week in the Altos data, with the 13-week average up 4.9% year-over-year and still rising — that underlying trend is more durable than the spot week suggests. The spot read for the week ending June 12 ticked down 0.7% and the four-week average has now fallen for four straight weeks, landing at +6.0% YoY. The divergence between the softening four-week trend and the still-rising 13-week average is worth watching: it may be normal late-spring seasonality, or it may be the first hint of rate fatigue. Active inventory reached 1.047 million units, down 1.1% year-over-year — the YoY gap has been narrowing for months and is now at its tightest since October 2023, meaning the supply relief that defined 2024-2025 is stalling. New listings accelerated, with the four-week average up 3.3% YoY and the 13-week average up 1.0% and rising; sellers are coming to market, but buyers are absorbing them efficiently enough to keep inventory lean. NAR existing home sales came in at a 4.17 million SAAR for May, up 3.2% year-over-year and well above the 3.85 million consensus — the biggest beat in several months. On an NSA basis, May closings were flat year-over-year, which is the honest read on actual transaction volume; the seasonally adjusted strength reflects favorable calendar alignment more than a fundamental acceleration. New home sales, by contrast, fell to 622,000 SAAR in April, down 11.9% YoY, with months supply at 9.4 — builders are sitting on finished inventory and the divergence with existing sales is now wide enough to matter for pricing. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.55% today, essentially unchanged over the past four weeks after drifting lower through April and early May. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.12 points — historically this spread runs around 1.7 points, meaning roughly 40 basis points of excess spread remain. If credit-market volatility continues to recede, that compression alone could push the 30-year toward the low-to-mid 6s without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications averaged +4.3% YoY on the four-week basis, but the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA by two to four weeks by capturing earlier-stage credit inquiries — turned negative YoY on its 13-week average and its spot week fell 1.4%. When MII diverges below MBA like this, the leading interpretation is that the application pipeline is thinning at the front end; watch whether MBA's four-week average follows lower over the next month. Housing starts missed badly in May at 1,177,000 SAAR versus a 1,350,000 consensus, driven by a 42% monthly drop in multifamily starts and a second consecutive monthly decline in single-family; single-family permits held flat at 886,000, but that series has been negative YoY for 24 consecutive months. Builders are completing the existing pipeline — single-family completions hit their lowest level since June 2011 — while pulling back on new groundbreakings. That combination argues against a meaningful resupply of new homes in 2026 and into 2027.

The macro backdrop this month was notably cleaner than the consensus feared. May payrolls came in at +172,000 against a +59,000 consensus, unemployment held at 4.3%, and average hourly earnings rose 3.45% year-over-year — income growth that, while not generous relative to home prices, is at least running ahead of the current 1.3% YoY pace of existing-home median prices. Core CPI printed +0.21% month-over-month, cooler than the +0.3% consensus, and shelter CPI rose 0.32% — its continued deceleration matters for housing because shelter is the largest single component holding core inflation above the Fed's comfort zone; as it fades, the Fed gains room to maneuver without the housing market doing any of the work. Core PCE for April came in at +0.24% MoM, also below consensus, putting the year-over-year rate at 3.29% — still above target but decelerating. The labor picture carries a note of caution underneath the solid headline numbers: the JOLTS hires rate fell to 3.2% and the quits rate slipped to 1.9%, both moving in the direction that historically precedes a softening job market by two to three quarters. More pointedly, the count of workers unemployed 27 weeks or longer rose 35.8% year-over-year to 1.988 million. This cohort is not a 2026 selling-season story — these households are not yet delinquent, and long-term unemployment does not translate immediately to distressed listings. But if the hires rate does not recover, you watch that number accumulate.
