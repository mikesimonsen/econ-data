# Monthly Housing Summary — June 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales rose 7.2% year-over-year in the week ending June 19, and the four-week average sits at +5.5% — the 54th consecutive week of positive year-over-year readings. The 13-week average has held above +5% and is still accelerating, which tells you the underlying demand trend remains intact even as the headline weekly number has softened over the past five weeks. Total active inventory reached 1.06 million, up just 0.15% year-over-year — the YoY gap has essentially closed, and seasonal accumulation is proceeding normally. New listings ran 92,504 for the week, nearly flat year-over-year on a spot basis (+0.5%) with the four-week average up 2.8%; the 13-week average has risen 14 consecutive weeks, meaning supply is gradually rebuilding but not flooding the market. Pending days on market ticked down to 70.7 days, still elevated year-over-year (+6.4%) but that gap has been decelerating for weeks. NAR's May existing home sales came in at 4.17 million SAAR — a meaningful beat against the 3.85 million consensus — up 3.2% year-over-year and accelerating, with the South and Midwest driving volume. New home sales told the opposite story: 580,000 SAAR in May, down 7.4% year-over-year, with months of supply at 10.3 — builders are sitting on completed inventory while starts fell sharply in May (1,177,000 SAAR, well below the 1,350,000 consensus). Single-family permits have been negative year-over-year for 24 consecutive months, and single-family completions are running at their lowest annual pace since June 2011; the pipeline is draining, not refilling. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.53% today, down modestly over the past month. The more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: 2.14 percentage points, still about 45 basis points wide of the long-run norm near 1.70. That excess spread is the latent affordability lever — if it compresses toward historical norms as credit markets settle, the 30-year could approach the low-to-mid 6s without the Fed moving at all. The leading indicators are sending a mixed signal heading into July: the MBA purchase index four-week average is up 3.85% year-over-year but decelerating, and the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA by two to four weeks by measuring actual credit-pull intent before loan applications are filed — flipped negative year-over-year for the first time this cycle, with its four-week average turning lower as well. That divergence is worth watching closely; when MII turns before MBA, agents typically have two to four weeks of notice before purchase application volume follows.

The macro picture in June offered more clarity than usual, and most of it was constructive for housing — with one exception that deserves attention. May payrolls came in at 172,000, well above the 59,000 consensus, and the unemployment rate held at 4.3% against expectations of 4.4%. An employed household is a household that can carry a mortgage, so the payroll beat matters directly. But the JOLTS data told a more cautious story: the hires rate fell to 3.2% and the quits rate slipped to 1.9%, both reversals from recent gains. Workers quitting voluntarily are workers confident enough to trade up — in housing or in employment — so a softening quits rate can be an early signal of reduced household formation and move-up demand. Inflation was the genuine positive surprise of the month: headline CPI rose 4.2% year-over-year in May but came in cooler than expected month-over-month, and core PCE — the Fed's preferred measure — printed at 3.4% year-over-year with a 0.32% monthly gain against a 0.3% consensus. Neither reading is near target, but the direction matters for rate expectations more than the level right now. Shelter CPI at +3.4% year-over-year continued its slow deceleration; as realized market rents (Zillow's ZORI rose 2.95% year-over-year in May) feed further into the lagged index, shelter CPI should keep pulling core inflation lower through the second half of the year. On the regional front, the March Case-Shiller data — which carries the usual two-month lag — confirmed the geographic split that has been building all year: Chicago gained 6.0% year-over-year, New York 4.0%, Boston 2.0%; meanwhile Dallas fell 1.7%, Denver fell 1.9%, Seattle fell 2.4%, and Tampa extended its negative year-over-year run to 17 months. Agents in constrained-inventory Midwest and Northeast markets are operating in a functionally different price environment from their counterparts in Sun Belt markets where supply rebuilt faster after 2022.
