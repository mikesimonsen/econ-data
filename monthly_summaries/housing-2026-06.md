# Monthly Housing Summary — June 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held positive for the 53rd consecutive week on the Altos 13-week average, which rose to 89,288 — up 4.9% year over year and still trending higher after 14 weeks of gains. The spot weekly read came in at 90,205, up 4.7% YoY, though it ticked down slightly from the prior week, and the 4-week average of 89,357 has drifted lower for four consecutive weeks at +6.0% YoY — worth watching but not yet a reversal of the underlying trend. Active inventory reached 1,046,775, down 1.1% YoY and at its lowest since October 2023, meaning the supply growth story that dominated 2024-2025 has quietly stalled. New listings on the 4-week average are running +3.3% YoY and accelerating on the 13-week read (+1.0% YoY, rising 14 weeks), so sellers are coming to market at a modest pace — just not fast enough to rebuild inventory meaningfully against current absorption. Pending days on market at 70.9 have fallen for 14 consecutive weeks, a clean signal of firming pace even as the absolute level remains elevated year over year (+7.2%). NAR's May existing home sales print of 4.17 million SAAR came in well above the 3.85 million consensus, up 3.2% year over year and accelerating — the strongest beat of this cycle. On an NSA basis, May 390,000 units matched a year ago exactly, so the SAAR strength reflects favorable seasonal adjustment; directionally it confirms demand is holding. New home sales diverged sharply: April's 622,000 SAAR fell 6.2% from March and sits 11.9% below a year ago, with months supply rising to 9.4 — builders are carrying more finished inventory than the resale market, and that overhang is likely suppressing new home prices even as median new home price ticked up. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.56% today, down 35 basis points year over year, but the mortgage-Treasury spread (the gap between the 30-year rate and the 10-year Treasury yield, which historically runs around 1.7 percentage points) is 2.09 points — still roughly 40 basis points wide of normal. That excess spread is the mechanical reason rates haven't fallen further even as the 10-year has held near 4.47%; if it compresses toward historical norms, the 30-year could fall to the low-to-mid 6s without any Fed action. The MBA purchase index 4-week average is +5.6% YoY and the 13-week average has been positive for 69 consecutive weeks, both constructive. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA by roughly 2-4 weeks by measuring credit-pull activity before applications are filed — tells a more cautious story: the 13-week MII average has fallen five consecutive weeks and sits at -2.3% YoY, while the 4-week average just turned up marginally. The divergence between a still-positive MBA read and a softening MII suggests the purchase application trend could decelerate in late June and early July. Watch whether the MII 4-week average sustains its uptick — that's the clearest early signal for July demand.

The macro backdrop is better than the consensus expected entering June, but contains an important internal tension. May payrolls printed at +172,000 against a 59,000 consensus — a clean beat that lifted the employment picture — and the unemployment rate held at 4.3%, also beating the 4.4% forecast. Wage growth at +3.45% YoY continues to outpace shelter CPI (+3.35% YoY), which is the quiet affordability tailwind that rarely makes headlines: real purchasing power for shelter is essentially flat rather than eroding. Core CPI came in at +2.82% YoY and core PCE at +3.29% for April, both printing below consensus, which keeps the door open for Fed rate cuts later this year — though neither is close enough to 2% to force the Fed's hand imminently. The mechanism for housing is straightforward: softer-than-expected inflation reduces upward pressure on the 10-year Treasury, and the 10-year is the primary driver of mortgage rates in the current environment. Where the picture darkens is in labor market quality rather than quantity. The JOLTS hires rate fell to 3.2% and quits fell to 1.9% — both near post-pandemic lows — signaling a labor market where workers feel less confident about voluntarily leaving jobs. That directly suppresses household formation and move-up purchase activity. More consequentially for the medium term: workers unemployed 27 weeks or longer reached 1,988,000, up 35.8% year over year and rising for two consecutive months. This cohort doesn't affect the current selling season, but it is the population that typically produces mortgage stress 6-12 months out — worth flagging to clients who ask about distressed inventory timelines.
