# Monthly Housing Summary — June 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held positive for the 53rd consecutive week in the Altos data, with the 13-week average running +4.9% year over year and still rising — the underlying trend remains constructive even as the four-week average has softened to +6.0% and ticked down four straight weeks. That near-term softening is worth watching but not yet alarming; the 13-week trend has been improving for 14 weeks and carries more signal than the weekly noise. Inventory tells a similar two-speed story: Altos active supply sits at roughly 1.05 million units, down 1.1% year over year — the YoY gap has been narrowing and is now at its tightest since October 2023, meaning the inventory relief that characterized 2024-25 is fading. New listings are firming, with the four-week average up 3.3% YoY and the 13-week average accelerating, so sellers are coming to market; the constraint is that buyers are absorbing supply almost as fast as it arrives. Pending days on market at 70.9 days are up 7.2% YoY, so homes are taking longer to contract than a year ago, but that gap has been narrowing for 14 weeks. NAR's May existing home sales print came in at 4.17 million SAAR — well above the 3.85 million consensus — and up 3.2% year over year, with the NSA count flat YoY, confirming the SAAR beat was partly seasonal-adjustment driven rather than a clean acceleration. New home sales diverged sharply: April's 622K SAAR reading fell 6.2% from March and 11.9% YoY, with months of supply at 9.4 — builders are sitting on elevated finished inventory and responding accordingly, with single-family starts falling for a second straight month to 882K and now down 7.1% YoY. The starts miss versus the 1,350K consensus was significant. Single-family permits at 886K are down 1.5% YoY and have been negative for 24 consecutive months, which means the pipeline builders are clearing will not be replenished at the same pace — a 2027 supply story, not a 2026 one. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.66% as of June 22, up modestly on the week but still 18 basis points below a year ago. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.16 points — elevated relative to the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points, and the four-week average is holding near 2.12. That gap represents latent affordability; if the spread compresses toward 1.85-1.90 without any move in the 10-year, the 30-year could fall to roughly 6.30-6.35% on spread compression alone, before any Fed action. MBA purchase applications (four-week average) are up 4.3% YoY, a positive trend now 70 weeks running, though the 13-week average has just begun to decelerate. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead the MBA by two to four weeks — flipped to barely positive YoY at +0.1% on the spot reading, but the 13-week average is still down 2.3% YoY and has fallen five straight weeks. That divergence between the MII trend and the MBA trend is the most important leading indicator to watch over the next four weeks: if the MII four-week average doesn't firm, the MBA purchase index likely softens into July.

The macro backdrop is providing more support to housing than the headlines suggest. May payrolls came in at +172K against a 59K consensus — a substantial beat — and the unemployment rate held at 4.3%, also better than expected. Wage growth of 3.45% YoY comfortably outpaces shelter CPI at 3.35%, which means real affordability from the income side is no longer deteriorating. Core CPI printed +0.21% month over month in May, below the 0.3% consensus, and Core PCE for April was similarly benign at +0.24%. Both readings give the Fed cover to hold without tightening further, which keeps the 10-year anchored — it sits at 4.48% today, essentially unchanged on the week. The piece of the labor market worth watching carefully is the long-term unemployed cohort: workers unemployed 27 weeks or more rose 8.5% month over month in May and are up 35.8% year over year, with median unemployment duration jumping to 11.6 weeks. This does not threaten the current selling season — these households are not yet delinquent — but if this cohort continues accumulating at this pace into late 2026, it will show up in early-stage delinquency data in 2027.
