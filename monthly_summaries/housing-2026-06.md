# Monthly Housing Summary — June 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held firmly positive through the four weeks ending June 19, running at +7.2% year-over-year on the spot read and +5.5% on the four-week average — a streak that has now extended to 54 consecutive weeks on the 13-week measure, which itself ticked up to +5.2% YoY and has been rising for 14 straight weeks. The demand signal is real and durable, though the four-week average has softened for five consecutive weeks, so the trend is decelerating even as it stays positive. Pending days on market fell for the 14th straight week to 70.7 days, still running about 6% above year-ago but with that gap narrowing, which suggests the market is gradually firming at the margin. Inventory reached 1.063 million active listings on Altos as of June 19, up a modest +0.15% YoY — a meaningful turn after months of negative YoY readings — while new listings came in at 92,504 on the spot week, +0.5% YoY but with the four-week average slipping to -2.9% for the week; the 13-week average of 93,139 is still up +1.3% YoY and has been climbing for 14 weeks, so supply is growing but not accelerating. NAR's May existing home sales print was the headline of the month: 4.17 million SAAR, up +3.2% YoY and well above the 3.85 million consensus — the second consecutive monthly gain, with the South (+6.0% YoY) and West (+5.6% YoY) leading. NSA sales were flat YoY at 390,000, which is the honest number for direction; the SAAR beat reflects seasonal adjustment, not a sudden reopening of the market. New home sales told a different story — May came in at 580,000 SAAR, down 7.5% YoY, with months supply jumping to 10.3, the highest in over a year and a half. Builders are sitting on completed inventory they cannot move at current prices, and single-family starts fell again in May to 882,000 SAAR (-7.1% YoY) while single-family completions hit their lowest level since June 2011. The pipeline is draining without being refilled. The 30-year fixed closed June 24 at 6.55%, down about 10 basis points on the day and roughly 27 basis points below a year ago; the spread to the 10-year Treasury sits at 2.15 percentage points against a historical norm near 1.7 points, meaning there is still meaningful room for rates to fall without any Fed move if lender risk appetite improves. MBA purchase applications ran +3.9% YoY on the four-week average through June 19, but the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks by capturing credit-pull activity before a formal application is filed — turned negative YoY this week at -2.0% spot and -1.1% on the four-week average. That divergence is worth watching closely: if the MII is signaling real demand softness rather than noise, MBA applications could follow lower into mid-July. Watch whether the MII stabilizes or continues to slide over the next four weeks.

The macro backdrop is genuinely mixed in ways that matter differently for housing. May payrolls came in at +172,000, well above the +59,000 consensus, and the unemployment rate held at 4.3% — both supportive of mortgage qualification and purchase intent in the near term. But the JOLTS data told a more cautious story underneath: the hires rate fell to 3.2% and the quits rate dropped to 1.9%, both year-over-year declines, which matters for housing because voluntary job changes are one of the strongest triggers for a home purchase. When workers stop switching jobs, transaction demand softens even if employment levels stay high. The long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — rose 8.5% month-over-month and is now up 35.8% year-over-year at 1.988 million people; this will not affect the current selling season but is the figure to track for delinquency risk in 2027. On inflation, May Core PCE printed at +3.4% YoY and +0.32% month-over-month, above the Fed's 2% target and above the +0.3% consensus; Core CPI came in softer at +2.8% YoY, but shelter CPI is still running at +3.4% YoY, which means the Fed's preferred inflation gauge is being kept elevated in part by lagged housing cost measures that the actual market has already corrected. The 10-year Treasury sits at 4.39%, and with Core PCE still elevated, the Fed has little near-term justification to move — which keeps the primary lever for rate relief on the spread side rather than the benchmark side.
