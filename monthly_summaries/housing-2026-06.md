# Monthly Housing Summary — June 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has now been positive year-over-year for 55 consecutive weeks, and the 4-week average sits at +4.4% YoY — still constructive, though decelerating from where it was earlier in the spring. The most recent weekly read dipped to -3.0% YoY as of June 26, so watch whether that single-week softness persists or reverts; the underlying trend remains positive. Total active inventory reached 1.07 million units, up 1.2% YoY — growth that has been decelerating meaningfully from the double-digit YoY gains of 2024-early 2025 — and new listings flipped to -7.1% YoY on the most recent week, though the 4-week average held barely positive at +0.4%. The 13-week average for new listings is still running +0.7% YoY and has risen for 14 consecutive weeks, suggesting the latest weekly dip is noise rather than a new trend. Pending days on market extended to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking longer to go under contract than a year ago, a texture worth noting at listing appointments even as demand remains net positive. NAR's May existing home sales print came in at 4.17 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a wide margin and running +3.2% YoY — the second consecutive monthly rise. On an NSA basis, May's 390,000 sales were flat YoY, which is the honest directional read: not accelerating, but not retreating. New home sales told a different story, falling to 580,000 SAAR in May, down 7.5% YoY, with months supply of new homes rising to 10.3 — builders are sitting on finished inventory that isn't clearing, and single-family starts fell a further 7.1% YoY. The 30-year fixed rate ended June at 6.52%, essentially unchanged on the month. The more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury, which closed at 2.14 percentage points against a 4.39% 10-year — still roughly 45 basis points above the long-run historical norm of about 1.7 points. The 4-week spread average has been rising for seven weeks, meaning rate relief has not arrived through the spread channel despite modest improvement in the 10-year. MBA purchase applications ran +3.9% YoY on the 4-week average through June 19 — positive but decelerating. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (a credit-inquiry-based read that leads MBA by 2-4 weeks) ticked up to a 4-week average of 127.5, reversing a recent decline, but its 13-week average remains down 2.4% YoY and has fallen for seven straight weeks. The divergence between a still-positive MBA trend and a still-negative MII 13-week trend is worth watching: if the MII doesn't firm further in July, MBA applications could soften into mid-summer. Single-family permits held at 892,000 SAAR — negative YoY for the 24th consecutive month — which tells you the new supply story beyond the current pipeline remains constrained.

The macro picture in June carried two important signals for housing and one slow-building risk. May payrolls came in at +172,000, well above the 59,000 consensus, and the unemployment rate held at 4.3% — an employed buyer base is the floor under current demand, and this print reinforces it. But the labor market internals are less clean than the headline: the hires rate dipped to 3.3% and the quits rate fell to 1.9%, both down YoY, which means workers feel less confident about voluntary mobility — and people who aren't changing jobs are among the least likely to buy homes. More sobering, the number of workers unemployed for 27 weeks or longer rose to 1.99 million, up 35.8% YoY, with median unemployment duration jumping to 11.6 weeks. This cohort doesn't affect the current selling season, but it accumulates toward future mortgage stress; watch it in the context of any softening in delinquency data later this year. On inflation, May core PCE came in at +3.4% YoY — still running above target — and core CPI printed +2.8% YoY, both cooler than feared but not at levels that give the Fed cover to ease. The 10-year Treasury ended June at 4.39%, and with the spread still at 2.14 points, the 30-year rate has limited room to fall unless either the 10-year rallies or lenders compress the spread. If the spread normalizes even 25 basis points toward historical norms without a Treasury move, the 30-year would approach the low 6s — that is the affordability lever to watch, and it remains closed for now.
