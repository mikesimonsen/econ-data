**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive but lost some momentum heading into the back half of summer. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks on a year-over-year basis, and the 4-week average stood at +4.4% YoY as of late June — though the most recent weekly print dipped to -3.0% YoY, a reminder that single-week readings are noisy at this stage of the seasonal calendar. Inventory continued to firm: Altos total active supply was up roughly 1.2% YoY as of late June, and Realtor.com active listings ran +2.1% YoY through July, though the pace of new listings has clearly decelerated — Altos new listings flipped to -7.1% YoY on the spot reading and the 4-week average thinned to near-flat (+0.4% YoY). Pending days on market ticked up 6.7% YoY, which is worth noting but not yet a red flag; homes are taking slightly longer to go under contract than a year ago, not dramatically longer. NAR's July existing home sales print of 4.06 million SAAR beat expectations (consensus was 3.85M) and showed a modest +1.3% YoY gain, though the headline masked a -0.7% month-over-month slip from June — the NSA series confirms the YoY read: up 2.8% from July 2025. New home sales (June, 628K SAAR) remained 5.1% below year-ago levels, and the average new-home sale price fell sharply — down 6.6% YoY — reflecting builders pricing aggressively to move inventory. NAR's existing-home inventory slipped to 1.54 million units and, notably, turned negative YoY for the first time after running positive for more than two years; months supply held at 4.6. The 30-year fixed sat at 6.77% today against a 10-year Treasury at 4.71%, putting the spread at roughly 2.06 percentage points — still well above the historical norm of around 1.7pp, but the 4-week average spread has been compressing for two consecutive weeks. If that compression continues toward 1.8-1.9pp, the 30-year could soften meaningfully without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications (4-week average) were -1.2% YoY as of August 14 — a mild deterioration from positive territory — and the 13-week average, while still up 0.8% YoY, has decelerated for six straight weeks. The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by 2-4 weeks, showed a spot reading of -5.6% YoY as of late June, which likely foreshadowed the MBA softness we are seeing now; the MII's 4-week average had reversed off its lows but the 13-week trend remains negative. The most consequential supply signal this month came from the starts/permits divergence: July starts collapsed to 1,239K SAAR, missing consensus by 111K and falling 12.8% YoY, with single-family starts down 15% YoY — yet permits rose to 1,443K SAAR, beating consensus and reaching their highest level since early 2024. Builders are pulling permits but not breaking ground, which implies they are reassessing near-term demand before committing to new pipeline. That pause in starts will constrain new-home deliveries in late 2026 and into early 2027. Watch whether the MII and MBA purchase index stabilize in the next four weeks; if both remain negative YoY through September, the fall selling season will open softer than the spring data suggested.

The macro picture is genuinely two-sided right now, and agents should resist reading it as simply good or bad. July payrolls came in at roughly -23K against a consensus of +59K — a notable miss that would ordinarily pressure rate expectations lower — but the unemployment rate simultaneously fell to 4.1% against a 4.4% expectation, and initial claims printed 206K for the week ending August 15, well below consensus and -12% YoY. What this tells you is that the labor market is not collapsing; it is rotating, with fewer new hires and fewer separations — a lower-velocity equilibrium. Average hourly earnings grew just 0.1% in July against a 0.3% expectation, which, combined with core CPI coming in at +2.5% YoY and core PCE at +3.3% YoY, keeps real wages positive but narrows the gap. The inflation side matters directly for housing: shelter CPI held at +3.2% YoY, still running above core, which means the Fed's preferred index will take time to converge. Soft payrolls plus soft wages plus below-consensus inflation have slightly reduced near-term pressure on the 10-year, which is why spread compression is the most actionable rate story right now — rates can ease without a Fed cut if the spread normalizes.
