**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held firmer than the spot numbers suggest. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been positive on a year-over-year basis for 55 consecutive weeks, running at roughly +4.8% YoY as of late June, and the 4-week average remained positive at +4.4% YoY even as the single-week read dipped to -3.0% — a normal seasonal soft patch, not a trend reversal. Inventory ticked up for eight straight weeks, with total Altos supply running about +1.2% YoY, but new listings decelerated sharply: the spot weekly reading turned negative YoY (-7.1%), and while the 4-week average is barely positive (+0.4%), the direction of travel is toward tighter supply, not looser. Pending days on market at 70.9 days remain elevated (+6.7% YoY), confirming that buyers are taking their time rather than competing urgently. NAR existing home sales for July printed at a 4.06 million SAAR — beating expectations of 3.85 million by a notable margin — though the headline fell modestly from June and is up only 1.3% YoY. New home sales for June came in at 628K SAAR, down 5.1% YoY, with median new-home prices off 2.7% YoY as builders continue to buy down rates and reduce mix to move product. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.75% today, up 16 basis points YoY, but the more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: at roughly 2.01 points currently versus a historical norm near 1.7 points, there is still meaningful room for the mortgage market to deliver affordability improvement independent of Fed action — if credit conditions ease or volatility falls. The spread has compressed about 24 basis points over the past year, which is the real affordability story most agents aren't tracking. On leading indicators: the MBA purchase index 4-week average is essentially flat YoY (-0.2%) after recovering from a brief pullback, while the 13-week average, though still positive at +1.7% YoY, has decelerated for five straight weeks. The Xactus MII — which tends to lead MBA applications by two to four weeks by capturing credit-pull activity before a formal application is filed — is running at -5.6% YoY on the spot and -2.3% on the 4-week average, diverging meaningfully from MBA's near-flat read. That divergence is worth watching: if the MII doesn't recover in the next two weeks, expect MBA purchase applications to soften into September. The most consequential supply signal of the month is the starts-vs.-permits divergence in today's Census release: July single-family starts fell 9.9% in a month and are down 15% YoY, badly missing expectations, while single-family permits rose 2.5% to their highest since May 2024. Builders are pulling back on ground-breaking while simultaneously authorizing more future work — a pattern that clears their existing pipeline, keeps near-term inventory tight, and sets up a modest supply expansion in 2027, not 2026.

The macro picture is cleaner than it looks. July payrolls missed badly — roughly -23K against a +59K consensus — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, beating expectations of 4.4%, and the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) ticked down for a second consecutive month to 1.77 million. That combination suggests the payroll miss may reflect survey noise or seasonal adjustment irregularities more than a genuine labor deterioration; when the long-term unemployed cohort is declining, the pipeline to mortgage stress is not building. Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% in July versus an expected 0.3%, which alongside a cooler-than-expected core CPI print of +0.2% MoM (2.47% YoY) and a core PCE that came in at +0.1% MoM against a 0.3% consensus keeps the Fed's hand on hold without forcing it. For housing, this matters through two channels: lower wage growth reduces inflationary pressure that would push the 10-year higher, and a Fed that isn't forced to hike preserves the rate plateau rather than breaking it upward. Shelter CPI continues to run at 3.2% YoY, above both headline and core — a lag indicator that will keep reported inflation elevated even as actual rent growth (Zillow ZORI at +3.0% YoY) is benign. The Case-Shiller data through May (released July 29) reinforces the geographic split that should inform every listing conversation: Chicago is up 6.9% YoY, New York +4.0%, Boston +2.6%, while Dallas is -1.0%, Denver -1.7%, Seattle -1.8%, Tampa -1.8%, and Phoenix -1.3%, all with negative YoY readings. Atlanta has essentially gone flat at -0.1% YoY. The Midwest and Northeast are carrying national appreciation; the Sun Belt metros that led the pandemic run-up are still correcting.
