**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive in aggregate through late June but showed the first cracks worth watching. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales was up 4.81% year-over-year and had run positive for 55 consecutive weeks — the underlying trend is intact. The 4-week average told a softer story, decelerating to +4.37% YoY, and the spot week for June 26 dipped to -2.99% YoY, the first negative weekly read in that streak. One week does not end a trend, but the deceleration from mid-spring is real. Inventory rose for eight consecutive weeks to just over 1.07 million units, up roughly 1.2% YoY, with new listings running slightly below year-ago levels on a 4-week basis after a brief positive stretch — new supply is not accelerating into the demand softness, which is the stabilizing factor. Pending days on market held elevated at 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, signaling that buyers remain deliberate. July existing home sales (SAAR 4.06 million) beat consensus by a wide margin and came in +1.25% YoY — a solid read — though NAR inventory ticked below year-ago for the first time in a while, flipping the YoY from positive to -0.65%. New home sales (June, 628K SAAR) were down 5.1% YoY with months supply at 9.3 — builders are still sitting on elevated stock. The 30-year fixed is at 6.77% as of August 21, essentially flat over the past four weeks. The more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: 2.06 points on the day, with the 4-week average compressing to 2.09 from above 2.30 a year ago. Historical norm is around 1.70 points. There is still 35-40 basis points of spread excess that could compress without any move in the 10-year — that is the path to a 6.3-6.4% rate that does not require a Fed cut. MBA purchase applications are a concern: the 4-week average was -1.20% YoY as of August 14, reversing from positive, and the 13-week average has fallen for six consecutive weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks — did reverse higher to -5.59% YoY on the spot week but remains deeply negative on the 13-week average at -2.38% YoY and still falling. The MII and MBA are not diverging in a hopeful way right now; they are both soft, with MII offering a tentative one-week uptick that needs confirmation. The July starts miss (1,239K vs. 1,350K expected, -12.4% month-over-month, -12.8% YoY) is the supply story of the month — but the permit read pushes back on alarm: total permits came in at 1,443K, beating expectations, up 5.95% YoY to the highest since January 2024, with single-family permits at their best since May 2024. Builders are pulling permits but not breaking ground, which implies they are managing pipeline carefully rather than abandoning the market. What to watch in the next four weeks: whether the Xactus MII uptick extends into July data (the next read will arrive before the September summary) and whether the MBA 4-week average stabilizes or continues to fade — that combination will tell us whether the late-June demand dip was seasonal noise or a genuine trend break.

The macro backdrop is genuinely mixed in ways that matter differently for housing. July payrolls missed by 82,000 jobs — actual -23K net change against a +59K consensus — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, beating expectations of 4.4%, and initial jobless claims for the week ending August 15 came in at 206,000, well below the 212K consensus and -12% YoY. The divergence between weak payrolls and a falling unemployment rate suggests the labor force itself is contracting slightly, not that layoffs are accelerating — which means the current homeowner base is not under acute payment stress, but income growth for would-be first-time buyers is not strengthening either. Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% in July versus the 0.3% expected, keeping real income gains modest. On inflation, July CPI core printed +0.22% month-over-month (+2.47% YoY) and core PCE for June came in at +0.13% MoM (+3.29% YoY) — both cooler than consensus. The shelter CPI component continues to rise (+0.14% MoM, +3.16% YoY), which creates an awkward feedback loop: shelter inflation is holding headline inflation above the Fed's preferred path, partly because actual home prices and rents are rising slowly, and partly because the index lags by six to twelve months. That lag means shelter CPI is unlikely to pull core PCE materially lower until late 2026 or early 2027, keeping the Fed patient even as goods inflation cools.
