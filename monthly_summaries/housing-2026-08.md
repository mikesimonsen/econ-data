**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of the summer, though the most recent Altos weekly read showed new pending sales dipping 3.0% below year-ago levels — the first negative YoY week in roughly 55 weeks, breaking a streak that had been one of the more durable demand signals of the past year. The four-week average still sits at +4.4% YoY, and the 13-week average, which is the cleaner signal, remains at +4.8% YoY with 14 consecutive weeks of gains — so the single negative week looks more like a seasonal wobble than a trend reversal, but it deserves watching. Inventory rose to roughly 1.07M active listings, up just over 1% YoY, with new listings decelerating: the spot week fell 7.1% YoY while the four-week and 13-week averages held just above flat. New listing supply is not building; it is fading into the fall. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking longer to clear, which is the one metric consistent with mild softening in the upper end of the market. NAR's July existing home sales print came in at 4.06M SAAR, up 1.25% YoY and well above the consensus estimate of 3.85M — the beat was genuine, not a revision artifact. NAR inventory at 1.54M units actually ticked down YoY for the first time in over two years, a sign that the supply build that defined 2025 may be stalling. New home sales told a starker story: July came in at 607K SAAR, down 10.5% from June and 5% below last July, with months of supply jumping to 9.6 — builders are sitting on finished inventory as starts collapsed 12.7% YoY in July, missing expectations by a wide margin. The starts-to-permits divergence is meaningful: total permits rose 5.2% YoY and single-family permits reached their highest level since May 2024, signaling builders intend to pull back completions in the near term while keeping optionality open for 2027. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.74% as of August 25, down slightly on the week. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.08 percentage points — still roughly 38 basis points above the long-run norm of about 1.70 — but the four-week spread average has been compressing, down 23 basis points YoY. If that compression continues another 30–35 basis points, the conforming rate approaches the mid-6s without the Fed doing anything. MBA purchase applications are the concern for next month: the four-week average is down 3.3% YoY and the 13-week average has been falling for seven consecutive weeks, flattening to essentially zero YoY growth. The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks, bounced in late June after a multi-week decline — that uptick has not yet shown up in MBA data, which means either the MII signal was a false start or MBA should firm modestly in early September. That divergence is the single most actionable leading indicator right now.

The macro backdrop in August was cleaner than it looked on the surface. July payrolls came in roughly 82,000 below consensus, but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% — beating a 4.4% consensus — and the long-term unemployed count (those out of work 27 or more weeks) actually dropped 8.6% in a single month to 1.77M, reversing what had been a quietly accumulating risk for future delinquency volumes. That cohort still bears watching over a six-to-twelve month horizon, but the July data at least stopped the bleed. Average hourly earnings grew just 0.1% in July against a 0.3% expectation, which, combined with core CPI printing at 2.47% YoY and core PCE at 3.29% YoY with a soft monthly read, gives the Fed more room than the rate market was pricing a month ago. Shelter CPI at 3.16% YoY continues its slow deceleration, and as observed rents (Zillow ZORI at +3.0% YoY) keep trending below the lagged shelter measure, that deceleration has further to run — which is disinflationary for core CPI, not inflationary. Consumer confidence at 90.8 beat expectations but sits 8% below last July, and that persistent gap between hard labor data (solid) and soft sentiment data (weak) is the tension that defines the current buying environment: qualified buyers exist, but willingness to commit is uneven. The regional price picture from Case-Shiller's May data (reported July 29) continues to show Chicago at +6.9% YoY and New York at +4.0% — both rising for six or seven consecutive months — while Dallas is down 1.0%, Denver down 1.7%, Phoenix down 1.3%, and Tampa down 1.8% YoY and still declining.
