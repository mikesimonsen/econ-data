**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive on the underlying trend even as the headline weekly number softened. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales is running +4.81% year over year and has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks — that streak is the clearest read on where the market actually stands. The most recent weekly spot print (week of June 26) dipped to -2.99% YoY after falling three straight weeks, but the 4-week average remained at +4.37%, which is where I'd anchor. Pending days on market edged to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, a reminder that homes are moving somewhat more slowly than a year ago even as volume holds. Inventory rose to roughly 1.07 million active listings, up about 1.2% YoY — growth is real but not accelerating, and the Realtor.com July read confirms it at 1.13 million, +2.1% YoY. New listings are the more cautionary signal: the Altos 4-week average turned slightly positive at +0.41% YoY, but the single-week reading fell to -7.1% YoY, and Realtor.com's July new listing count is at its lowest since October 2023. Sellers are not flooding the market. NAR existing home sales printed 4.09 million SAAR for June, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a wide margin and running +4.1% YoY — that acceleration is notable. On a non-seasonally-adjusted basis, the year-over-year gain was +7.9%, with every region positive. New home sales (628K SAAR, -5.1% YoY) tell a different story: builders are clearing completed inventory at modest price concessions — the new-home median fell to $398,300, down 2.7% YoY — while single-family permits slipped in June even as starts jumped, driven almost entirely by a multifamily spike. That starts-vs.-permits divergence implies builders are completing what's in the pipeline but not committing to much new single-family supply. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.76% as of August 10, essentially flat over the past month after edging up two basis points this week. The more important number is the spread to the 10-year Treasury: 2.11-2.12 points, down 21-22 basis points year over year. The historical norm is roughly 1.7 points, so the spread remains elevated, but its compression over the past year means rates have fallen more than the 10-year alone would imply — and any further normalization toward 1.9 points would move the 30-year meaningfully lower without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications tell a more cautious story: the 4-week average is -0.23% YoY and has been falling for five weeks, with the most recent week printing -2.53% YoY — the first negative YoY reading in 77 weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (which leads MBA by roughly 2-4 weeks by capturing credit-inquiry activity before borrowers formally apply) gave a partial counterpoint: its 4-week average turned back up in late June, which suggests the MBA softness may stabilize rather than deepen in August. Watch whether that MII uptick follows through into the next MBA reads — that's the most actionable leading indicator right now.

The macro backdrop shifted in two meaningful directions this month, and they point in opposite directions for housing. The July payroll print was the bigger surprise: nonfarm payrolls came in at -23,000 against a consensus of +59,000, a miss of over 80,000 jobs. That alone would normally read as a warning signal for purchase demand, since job security is the primary driver of first-time buyer decisions. But the unemployment rate simultaneously fell to 4.1% (expected 4.4%), initial claims came in at 199,000 against an expectation of 212,000, and the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) dropped 8.6% in a single month after ticking up. Taken together, the picture is a labor market that may be producing fewer new jobs but isn't generating the layoffs that would impair existing homeowners' ability to pay or prospective buyers' willingness to commit. The wage number was soft — average hourly earnings rose only 0.05% month over month against a 0.3% expectation — which reduces wage-inflation pressure on the Fed. On inflation, core PCE came in at +0.13% for June (expected +0.3%), and core CPI was essentially flat (-0.02%). Shelter CPI is still running +3.26% YoY, providing the main floor under core inflation, but private-market rent data is moving the other way: the Zillow Observed Rent Index accelerated to +3.34% YoY in June — its highest since June 2023 — which could eventually feed back into shelter CPI in 12-18 months. For now, the cooling in core PCE and earnings keeps the Fed on hold rather than hiking, and rate futures are not pricing in near-term cuts either.
