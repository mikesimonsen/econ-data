**August 2026 Housing Summary**

The most important demand story this month is also the most easily misread: new pending sales on a spot-week basis went negative year-over-year at the end of June, but the 4-week average held at +4.4% and the 13-week average sits at +4.8% YoY, positive for 55 consecutive weeks. That streak is the signal; the spot dip is seasonal noise. The 13-week average has been rising for 14 weeks, which tells you the underlying demand trend has not broken — it has simply decelerated from the firmer pace of late spring. Inventory continued its slow climb, with Altos total active listings up roughly 1% YoY and Realtor.com active count up 2.1% YoY in July, but new listings growth is softening: the Altos 13-week new listings average is up only 0.7% YoY after running positive for several months, and Realtor.com new listings fell 2.6% YoY in July to their lowest level since October 2023. Pending days on market sat at 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking longer to go under contract, but the buyer pool has not evaporated. NAR's July existing home sales printed at 4.06 million SAAR, beating the 3.85 million consensus by a meaningful margin; the NSA read was up 2.8% YoY, the sixth consecutive positive YoY month. The caveat is that new home sales told a different story — July came in at 607,000 SAAR, down 10.5% on the month and 5% YoY, with months supply jumping to 9.6. Builders are sitting on a growing completed-home inventory and cutting median prices (-0.9% YoY), which is the sign of a segment clearing excess supply rather than expanding. The rate picture is not helping: the 30-year fixed ended August at 6.81%, up six basis points on the month, and the spread to the 10-year Treasury widened back to 2.14 points on the latest daily read — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points. The 4-week spread average has been compressing over the year (-21bp YoY), meaning some of last year's rate relief came from the spread narrowing rather than Treasury moves. That compression has stalled. MBA purchase applications were down 3.3% YoY on the 4-week average, and the 13-week average, though barely positive (+0.02% YoY), has been falling for seven weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA by two to four weeks by capturing early-stage credit pulls before applications are filed — bounced off its recent low in late June (+3.5% on the week, 4-week average reversing higher), which was a tentative positive signal. Watch whether that MII uptick translates into MBA stabilization in the September data; if it does not follow through, the demand deceleration is more durable than the 13-week pending average implies.

The macro backdrop is genuinely two-sided this month, and agents should not let either the good or the bad headline run away with the narrative. July payrolls missed badly — the establishment survey came in roughly 23,000 below the prior month versus a consensus expectation near +59,000 — but the household survey told a different story: the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, beating the 4.4% consensus, and long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) dropped 8.6% on the month to 1.771 million, reversing a worrying multi-month climb. That cohort matters to housing not because it affects who is buying today, but because it is the leading indicator of future mortgage distress; its reversal is modestly encouraging for 6–12 month delinquency risk. Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% in July versus an expected 0.3%, which matters for affordability math in both directions — less wage growth supports the Fed's patience, but also means real purchasing power for first-time buyers is grinding rather than growing. Inflation data reinforced the patience thesis: July core CPI came in at +0.2% MoM, below the 0.3% consensus, and core PCE for July printed at +0.25% MoM, keeping the YoY at 3.34% — still above the Fed's 2% target but no longer accelerating. Shelter CPI at +3.2% YoY continues to run above overall core inflation, which keeps measured inflation elevated even as market rents (Zillow ZORI: +3.0% YoY) tell a softer story. The 10-year Treasury held at 4.67% as of August 28, and with the spread still near 2.1 points, meaningful rate relief for buyers requires either Treasury yields to fall or the spread to compress — neither of which is assured by current conditions.
