**August 2026 Housing Summary**

The most important demand signal this month is the one that's softening: the Altos 13-week average for new pending sales held a 55-week positive YoY streak through late June, but the four-week average decelerated to +4.4% YoY and the spot week turned negative at -3.0% YoY — the first negative weekly print in over a year. That single-week read shouldn't be over-weighted given seasonal noise in late June, but combined with the 13-week average rolling over, it signals that the demand expansion is losing momentum rather than building on it. Altos inventory rose for an eighth consecutive week and sat just above 1.07 million active listings, up about 1.2% YoY — growth that has visibly decelerated from the double-digit YoY gains of late 2024. New listings turned negative YoY on a spot basis (-7.1%) though the four-week average barely held positive (+0.4%), suggesting sellers are pulling back into the slower late-summer period faster than usual. Pending days on market at 70.9 days is running 6.7% above last year, meaning homes are sitting longer even as inventory growth stalls — a combination that points to buyer hesitation more than supply pressure. NAR's July existing home sales came in at 4.06 million SAAR, up 1.25% YoY and well above the 3.85 million consensus, though it slipped slightly from June's revised read; the headline beat is real but the month-over-month direction is worth watching. The stark divergence with new home sales — which fell to 607,000 SAAR in July, down 5% YoY with months supply jumping to 9.6 — reflects builders absorbing the rate environment differently than the resale market. On rates: the 30-year fixed closed August at 6.87%, up about 37 basis points YoY, and the spread to the 10-year Treasury sits at roughly 2.08-2.11 points — still well above the historical norm of around 1.7 points, but down about 20 basis points from a year ago. That compression is the hidden affordability story: if the spread normalizes even partially, meaningful rate relief is possible without any Fed action. The leading indicators lean cautious. The MBA purchase index four-week average is running -3.3% YoY and has fallen for two consecutive weeks; the Xactus MII — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks — was still negative YoY at -5.6% as of late June despite a recent bounce in its four-week average, suggesting the MBA softness extends into September. Watch whether the Altos 13-week pending average holds positive YoY as the comparison period toughens; a break below zero would be the clearest demand warning sign of the year. One supply signal worth noting: July single-family permits rose to 894,000 SAAR, the highest since May 2024, even as starts fell sharply to 808,000 — builders are authorizing future supply while pulling back on breaking ground, a pattern that has no near-term inventory implication but matters for 2027 supply.

The macro backdrop in August sent conflicting signals that matter differently for housing. July payrolls missed badly — the economy added roughly 23,000 net jobs against a 59,000 consensus — but the unemployment rate simultaneously fell to 4.1%, well below the 4.4% expected, and initial claims came in at 203,000 against a 212,000 forecast. The payroll miss is concerning for purchase confidence, but the claims and unemployment rate together say the labor market is softening in hiring rather than deteriorating into layoffs — a distinction that matters because households don't sell or defer buying when they feel their job is secure; they defer when they fear losing it. The long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) actually fell by 8.6% in July to 1.77 million — a genuine positive reversal after several months of accumulation — which reduces the near-term delinquency pressure that had been building. On inflation, July core PCE printed at 3.34% YoY, still running above the Fed's target and up from prior months, while core CPI came in cooler than expected at +2.47% YoY. The shelter CPI component held at 3.16% YoY — the lag between real-time rent data and measured shelter inflation is still working its way through, and Zillow's ZORI at +3.0% YoY suggests that lag won't compress the shelter component meaningfully until late 2026. With the 10-year Treasury at 4.76% and core PCE above 3.3%, the Fed has little room to cut without appearing to accommodate inflation — which is why spread compression rather than rate cuts is the more plausible near-term affordability relief mechanism.
