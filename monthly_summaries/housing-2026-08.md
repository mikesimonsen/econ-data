**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held its footing in July even as the spot data turned choppy. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales is running roughly 5% above year-ago levels and has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks — the underlying trend is intact despite the most recent weekly reading dipping 3% YoY, a move that looks more seasonal than structural when the four-week average (+4.4% YoY) is alongside it. Inventory continued rising, with Altos total active listings up about 1% YoY, though new listings fell back into negative YoY territory at the spot level; the 13-week average for new listings is still modestly positive, suggesting the pullback is decelerating rather than reversing. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking a little longer to clear, but not alarmingly so. NAR's July existing home sales came in at a 4.06 million SAAR, up 1.25% YoY and well ahead of the 3.85 million consensus, though the print fell slightly from June — typical seasonal behavior. The Northeast continues to outperform on both volume and price appreciation; the South is flat YoY on sales and median price growth there is the softest of the four regions. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.74% as of August 12, down modestly from recent highs. The more important development is the spread between the 30-year and the 10-year Treasury, which has compressed to roughly 2.06 percentage points — still above the long-run norm of about 1.7 percentage points, but the tightest it has been in over a year. Every 10 basis points of additional compression translates to a lower rate without any Fed action. The MBA purchase index four-week average is essentially flat YoY (–0.2%), a significant improvement from where it was sitting earlier in the summer. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks — turned up in late June after a seven-week slide in its 13-week average; that uptick has not yet fully shown up in the MBA series, which means the purchase application data over the next four weeks should be the primary thing to watch.

The macro backdrop shifted more than it appeared to. July payrolls missed badly — the economy added roughly 23,000 jobs against a consensus of 59,000 — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, well below the 4.4% expected, and the long-term unemployed cohort (those out of work 27+ weeks) actually declined to 1.77 million, reversing several months of accumulation. That matters because it is the long-term unemployed cohort, not the headline rate, that eventually feeds mortgage delinquency pipelines; a reversal here, if it holds, is a quiet positive for credit quality over the next 6–12 months. Wage growth came in at 3.15% YoY but only 0.1% month-over-month, cooler than expected, and July CPI printed at 3.3% YoY with core at 2.47% — both softer than forecast. Core PCE for June was 3.29% YoY, also below consensus. The combined picture is one where inflation is decelerating without a labor market unraveling, which is the environment most likely to let the mortgage-Treasury spread compress further. On the regional price story: Case-Shiller data through May (the index lags by roughly two months) shows Chicago up nearly 7% YoY, New York up 4%, and Boston accelerating to +2.6% — while Dallas, Denver, Phoenix, Tampa, and Seattle are all still negative YoY and in most cases still falling on a monthly basis. That split is widening, not narrowing. Agents in Midwest and Northeast markets are operating in a fundamentally different pricing environment than Sun Belt colleagues, and the supply data — Southern permits down 8.7% YoY in June, Midwest permits up — suggests the divergence has more runway. The macro signal worth watching next month: whether the September Fed meeting language shifts in response to cooling inflation and the soft payroll print, and whether that moves the 10-year enough to bring the 30-year meaningfully below 6.5%.
