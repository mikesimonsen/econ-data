# Monthly Housing Summary — August 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The four weeks ending August 11 present a picture that is more nuanced than the "housing recovery is stalling" narrative you may have heard. Altos weekly demand shows the 13-week average of new pending sales running +4.81% year-over-year — positive for 55 consecutive weeks — even as the most recent spot reading dipped to -2.99% YoY and the 4-week average decelerated to +4.37%. That week-over-week softness looks like normal late-summer seasonality rather than a structural turn; the streak and the 13-week trend are the more reliable signal. Inventory at Altos stood at roughly 1.07 million units as of June 26, up just +1.2% YoY — a deceleration from earlier in the year — and new listings on a 4-week basis were essentially flat YoY (+0.41%), with the spot week turning negative (-7.1% YoY). That new-listings fade is tightening available supply faster than the headline inventory number suggests. Pending days on market at 70.9 days is running +6.7% YoY, confirming that homes are sitting slightly longer than a year ago, though not long enough to shift negotiating leverage materially. NAR existing home sales for June printed at 4.09 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million by a wide margin and running +4.1% YoY — the strongest beat in several months, even as the month-over-month reading ticked down from May. NSA existing sales were up +7.9% YoY in June, with every region positive. New home sales at 628K SAAR were also up month-over-month but remain -5.1% YoY, and new home prices are falling (-2.7% YoY median, -6.6% average), a signal that builders are buying down rates and trimming specs to move inventory. Permits tell the more important supply story: single-family permits at 872K remain barely positive YoY (+0.7%), while the June multifamily starts spike to 513K (5+ units) looks like catch-up from prior permit delays rather than a genuine acceleration — multifamily permits are actually down -5.9% YoY and falling. The starts-permits divergence means future single-family supply is not growing. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.76% as of August 10, ticking up two basis points after a recent decline, with the spread to the 10-year Treasury at 2.06 points on the daily read (weekly average 2.12 points). The historical norm on that spread is roughly 1.7 points, so there is still 35-40 basis points of compression available before rates reach "normal" pricing — meaning rates could fall to the low-to-mid 6s without any Fed action if credit and volatility conditions ease. The MBA purchase index 4-week average is -0.23% YoY, essentially flat but with a deteriorating trend over five weeks. More importantly, the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA applications by two to four weeks — turned up on its 4-week average (+2.31%) after recent declines, with the YoY trend improving even though the index remains -2.3% YoY. That divergence between a firming MII and a softening MBA suggests purchase application activity may stabilize or tick up in late August. Watch whether the MII 13-week average, currently falling for seven consecutive weeks at -2.4% YoY, confirms or contradicts that near-term bounce.

The macro backdrop for housing shifted in a meaningful way this month. The July payroll report missed consensus by roughly 80,000 jobs (actual -23K net revision context aside, the establishment survey printed well below the +59K expectation), and average hourly earnings rose only +0.1% against an expected +0.3% — together these soften the "labor market is too strong for rates to fall" argument that had been keeping the 10-year elevated. The unemployment rate actually fell to 4.1% against a consensus of 4.4%, and long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) pulled back to 1.771 million after rising earlier this year — a meaningful reversal worth watching because that cohort is the leading indicator for future mortgage stress; its decline removes some tail risk from the distressed-inventory story for now. Core PCE for June came in at +0.13% month-over-month against an expected +0.3%, putting the year-over-year at 3.29% — still above the Fed's 2% target but decelerating, and the soft earnings and payroll data reinforce that the disinflationary path remains intact. The mechanism for housing is direct: if core PCE continues to cool and labor softens without breaking, the Fed has more room to hold or cut, which compresses the 10-year and creates the conditions for spread normalization to do the affordability work. The 10-year Treasury ticked back up to 4.70% as of August 10, reversing a recent decline, so the near-term rate environment is not getting easier — but it is also not deteriorating structurally.
