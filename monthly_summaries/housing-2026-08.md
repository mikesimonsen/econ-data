**August 2026 Housing Summary**

The most important demand signal of the month is the one that's easy to misread: new pending sales on a spot-week basis turned negative year-over-year for the first time in over a year, but the 4-week average held at +4.4% YoY and the 13-week average remains at +4.8% YoY — positive for 55 consecutive weeks. One soft week in late June does not break a 55-week streak, and the 4-week trend says the underlying contract flow is still running ahead of last year. Inventory at 1.07 million active listings was up about 1.2% YoY in late June, a meaningful deceleration from the double-digit growth rates of 2024, and new listings have stalled — the 4-week average is barely positive YoY and the spot week turned negative. That tightening supply picture explains why pending days on market, at 70.9 days, is running roughly 7% above last year but not accelerating; homes are taking longer to sell than in the frenzy years, but the pace of lengthening is not worsening. July existing home sales printed at 4.06 million SAAR, up 1.25% YoY and well above the 3.85 million consensus — a clean beat, though the month-over-month dip is normal seasonal behavior and the NSA YoY of +2.8% is the more reliable read. New home sales (June, 628K SAAR) remain down 5% YoY as builders absorb a 9.3-month supply overhang and cut prices — average new home prices fell nearly 6.6% YoY, an unusually sharp move that signals active incentive use. The 30-year fixed rate ticked back up to 6.71% as of August 14 after several weeks of softening, with the 10-year Treasury at 4.71% — a spread of roughly 2.05-2.08 basis points. That spread has compressed about 20-22 basis points from a year ago, which is the hidden affordability story: even without a Fed move, if the spread normalizes further toward its long-run average near 1.7 points, the 30-year could fall 35 basis points from current levels without the 10-year moving at all. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA purchase applications by 2-4 weeks — showed its 4-week average at -2.3% YoY as of late June, running notably weaker than the MBA purchase index 4-week average which is now essentially flat YoY (-0.2%). That divergence is worth watching: the MII has historically flagged softness before it shows up in MBA data, and if it doesn't recover in the next reading, September application volume may disappoint. Single-family permits at 872K are turning positive YoY for the first time since May 2024, which matters for 2027 supply, but 5-plus unit permits fell 5.9% YoY and South permits dropped 8.6% — the multifamily pullback is real and will tighten rental supply before it tightens for-sale supply.

The macro picture is genuinely mixed in ways that pull in different directions for housing. July payrolls came in 82,000 light of consensus (+23K net revision vs. +59K expected), but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% against a 4.4% consensus — a combination that suggests the labor market is cooling at the margins without breaking. The long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) actually fell 8.6% in July to 1.77 million, reversing a concerning build from prior months; that reduces near-term delinquency pressure, though the cohort remains elevated enough to watch. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1% in July versus a 0.3% expectation, and core CPI printed at +2.47% YoY with a 0.22% monthly gain — cooler than feared. Core PCE, the Fed's preferred measure, was at 3.29% YoY through June, still above target, but the monthly pace has been running at 0.1-0.2%. The mechanism for housing: softer wage growth and cooling inflation give the Fed less reason to hold rates higher-for-longer, which anchors the 10-year and in turn limits how much further the 30-year can rise from here. Shelter CPI at +3.16% YoY continues to lag the real-time rent data — Zillow's ZORI is at +3.0% YoY — meaning shelter will keep core inflation modestly elevated even as actual rent growth softens, a timing artifact that the Fed is aware of but that will drag in the reported numbers for several more months. On the regional price picture: the Case-Shiller May data (the most recent available, with its typical two-month lag) shows Chicago at +6.9% YoY and New York at +4.0% YoY, while Dallas is -1.0%, Denver -1.7%, Las Vegas -1.9%, Tampa -1.8%, and Seattle -1.8%.
