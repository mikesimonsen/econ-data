**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of the summer, though the most recent Altos weekly read showed new pending sales slipping 3.0% below year-ago levels for the week ending June 26 — the first negative YoY print after 55 consecutive weeks of positive readings. The four-week average remained in positive territory at +4.4% YoY, which is the more reliable signal, and the 13-week average is still running +4.8% YoY after 55 weeks of uninterrupted positive comparisons. That 13-week trend is what matters most heading into fall: it has decelerated from its spring peak but has not reversed. Inventory continues to build — total active supply was up roughly 1.2% YoY in late June per Altos, and Realtor.com's July active count is up 2.1% YoY, now running positive for 33 consecutive months. New listings, however, are pulling back: the Altos 4-week average for new listings turned only barely positive YoY (+0.4%) and the weekly spot read went negative (-7.1% YoY), so the inventory growth that's occurring is largely a function of homes sitting longer, not fresh supply flooding in. Pending days on market at 71 days are running about 7% above last year. NAR's July existing home sales came in at 4.06 million SAAR — above the 3.85M consensus by a wide margin — but that headline obscures a softer underlying picture: the NSA YoY read of +2.8% is respectable yet came off a slight sequential decline, and NAR's own inventory measure flipped to -0.65% YoY for the first time in over two years, a meaningful reversal. New home sales (June, 628K SAAR) ticked up from the prior month but remain 5% below last year, and builder average prices fell sharply. The July starts print is the number to sit with: total starts dropped to 1,239K SAAR, missing expectations by over 100K and falling 12.7% YoY — the South alone is at its lowest since April 2023. Permits told the opposite story, rising 6% YoY to their highest since January 2024, with single-family permits at a two-year high. That permits-versus-starts gap tells you builders are authorized to build but pulling back on breaking ground — they are managing existing pipeline in a rate environment that still pressures buyer affordability. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.72% as of August 19, down a few basis points over the month as the 10-year Treasury eased to 4.65%. The mortgage-Treasury spread — the gap between the 30-year fixed and the 10-year, which historically runs around 1.7 percentage points — is at 2.07 points, still about 37 basis points above normal. The weekly average has been compressing gradually and is now at 2.03 points on a three-week falling trend. If that compression continues another 20-30 basis points, effective rates move meaningfully lower without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications (4-week average) are running -1.2% YoY as of August 14 — a reversal from the mildly positive reads of early summer — and the 13-week average has fallen six consecutive weeks though it remains barely positive at +0.8% YoY. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index, which tends to lead MBA by 2-4 weeks, bounced in late June after a multi-week decline, with the 4-week average turning up +2.3%. That divergence — MII firming while MBA softens — suggests the MBA weakness may be closer to a seasonal air pocket than a trend break, but it is worth watching the August MBA reads carefully to see whether the MII signal was predictive.

The macro backdrop shifted in ways that matter for housing. July payrolls came in at roughly -23K against a +59K consensus — a notable miss that reflects both the soft labor environment and residual tariff-related uncertainty in goods-producing sectors. The unemployment rate, however, fell to 4.1%, beating expectations of 4.4%, and the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) ticked down to 1.77 million after several months of accumulation — a modest but real improvement in what had been a growing delinquency-risk cohort. The mechanism is straightforward: a household where one earner loses a job and stays unemployed for six months or more becomes a credit risk within 12-18 months; that cohort had been building through spring and a two-month reversal is encouraging even if one data point does not reset the concern.
