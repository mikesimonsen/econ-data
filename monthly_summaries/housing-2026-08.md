**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of the summer but is showing its first meaningful cracks in this cycle. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been positive year-over-year for 55 consecutive weeks, and the 4-week average sat at +4.4% YoY through late June — but the most recent weekly read turned negative (-3.0% YoY), ending what had been a constructive streak at the spot level. That divergence between the smoothed trend and the weekly print is worth watching closely: the 13-week average is still rising, which suggests the softness is not yet a trend reversal, but it does indicate demand decelerated into peak season. Inventory on the Altos side continued rising — up roughly 1.2% YoY through late June and climbing for eight consecutive weeks — though new listings turned negative YoY (-7.1% on the spot read, +0.4% on the 4-week average), which means the inventory build is slowing as sellers pull back. Pending days on market reached 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, a 103-week streak of year-over-year increases that tells you buyers have more negotiating time than they've had in years. NAR existing home sales printed 4.06 million SAAR in July, +1.3% YoY and meaningfully ahead of the 3.85 million consensus — the positive surprise matters — but the print still represented a second consecutive monthly decline from the spring peak. New home sales told a sharper story: 607,000 SAAR in July, down 10.5% on the month and -5.0% YoY, with months' supply jumping to 9.6. Builders are sitting on finished inventory and cutting prices — median new home price fell to $393,800, down 0.9% YoY. The starts/permits divergence is the supply signal to file away for 2027: total starts fell 12.4% in July and missed badly (1,239K vs. 1,350K expected), with single-family starts down 15% YoY, but total permits rose 4.3% and beat expectations, with single-family permits at their highest since May 2024. Builders are replenishing their pipelines even as they complete less right now — that implies a supply rebuild 12-18 months out, not today. The 30-year fixed rate stands at 6.78% as of August 24, up 24 basis points year-over-year and essentially unchanged on the month. The 30Y-10Y spread has been compressing gradually — the weekly average sits around 2.03 points, down 25 basis points YoY, against a historical norm near 1.7 points. There is still room to run on spread compression; if it continues toward historical norms, mortgage rates can fall without the Fed moving at all. The leading indicators are mixed and worth flagging: MBA purchase applications came in at -3.4% YoY on the spot read and -1.2% on the 4-week average, both of which just turned negative for the first time in several weeks. The Xactus MII — which tends to lead MBA by 2-4 weeks by capturing mortgage credit inquiries upstream of application — ticked up in late June after a recent decline, with the 4-week average also reversing higher, but the 13-week average remains negative YoY (-2.4%), down for seven consecutive weeks. That 13-week trend in the MII is the signal to watch through September: if it keeps falling while MBA tracks it lower with a short lag, purchase application volume will be the confirming data point.

The macro backdrop in August is better for housing than the headlines suggest, though the improvement is uneven. July payrolls missed consensus by roughly 80,000 jobs, but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% — two tenths below expectations — and long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) declined for a second consecutive month to 1.77 million, reversing a concerning accumulation trend from earlier this year. That matters because long-term unemployment is the leading indicator for mortgage delinquencies 6-12 months out; its reversal reduces near-term stress risk for the owned housing stock. Wage growth came in at +3.2% YoY, cooler than expected, which is simultaneously good news for inflation and a modest headwind for purchasing power at current price levels. On inflation: core CPI printed +2.5% YoY in July and core PCE was +3.3% YoY in June — both running above the Fed's target but decelerating, and both came in cooler than consensus.
