**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive but is decelerating. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been running above year-ago levels for 55 consecutive weeks, and the 4-week average was up roughly 4.4% YoY as of late June — but the most recent weekly read turned slightly negative (-3.0% YoY), a signal worth watching rather than panicking over, since single-week readings are noisy at seasonal turns. Inventory continued to expand — Altos total active supply was up about 1.2% YoY and rising for eight consecutive weeks — while new listings decelerated, with the 4-week average flipping to essentially flat YoY after several months of growth. That combination suggests the supply build is slowing, not reversing. Pending days on market held elevated at 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — not a sign of a seized market, but consistent with buyers having more negotiating room than they did a year ago. NAR's July existing sales came in at 4.06 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million, though that headline obscures a modest sequential pullback; on a YoY NSA basis, sales were up 2.8%, extending a six-month positive streak. New home sales (628K SAAR in June) remain about 5% below year-ago levels, and builders are clearly feeling the pressure: July housing starts fell to 1.239 million SAAR, missing expectations by roughly 111K and turning negative YoY for the first time in several months, with single-family starts down 15% YoY. The permits picture is more constructive — total permits rose to 1.443 million SAAR, beating expectations, with single-family permits up 2.2% YoY and at their highest level since May 2024. Read together, builders are permitting future supply while clearing current pipeline more slowly; that divergence is a 2027 story for inventory, not a 2026 one. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.72% today, ticking down three basis points over the period. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.07 percentage points — still well above the historical norm of roughly 1.70 points, meaning meaningful rate relief is available without the Fed moving a single time, purely through spread compression. MBA purchase applications averaged about 1.2% below year-ago levels over the past four weeks, and the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks — turned up in late June after several weeks of decline, with its 4-week average rising 2.3%, though still down 2.3% YoY. That MII uptick is the one leading signal pointing toward a possible stabilization in applications heading into September; watch whether it holds or reverses in the next two readings.

The macro backdrop was notably cleaner this month than it looked on the surface. July payrolls came in at roughly -23K against a consensus expectation of +59K, a miss that would normally unsettle markets — but the unemployment rate simultaneously fell to 4.1%, beating an expectation of 4.4%, and average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% month-over-month versus an expected 0.3%. The payroll miss reads less like labor-market deterioration and more like compositional noise when paired with the unemployment drop; the quits rate held at 2.0% and hires ticked up, both consistent with a labor market that has cooled but not broken. For housing, that matters because job security is what converts mortgage pre-approvals into signed contracts. The inflation data reinforced the soft-landing framing: core CPI came in at +0.2% month-over-month (expected +0.3%), core PCE at +0.1% (expected +0.3%), and headline CPI at +0.1% — all well below consensus. Shelter CPI rose 0.14% in July and is up 3.2% YoY, continuing its slow deceleration from the pandemic peak; that deceleration feeds directly into core PCE over the next several months as the BLS lagged rent methodology catches up to real-time data, which should give the Fed additional room to ease without declaring victory on inflation. The 10-year Treasury fell nine basis points to 4.65%, and if the spread were to compress even 20-25 basis points toward historical norms, the 30-year fixed would approach the low-to-mid 6.4% range — a threshold that has historically unlocked a meaningful cohort of rate-locked sellers. The long-term unemployed count (those out of work 27+ weeks) fell for a second consecutive month to 1.77 million; that eases, for now, the concern about a future delinquency pipeline building. The macro signal to track next month: whether core PCE's September print (covering July activity) confirms the disinflation trend, and whether the 10-year holds below 4.65% — because that combination, more than anything the Fed says at Jackson Hole, is what determines where the 30-year rate sits when September contracts close.
