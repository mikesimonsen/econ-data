**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive over the past four weeks, though the trend is softening at the edges. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales remains up 4.81% YoY — positive for 55 consecutive weeks — but the spot reading for the week of June 26 slipped to -2.99% YoY, the first negative weekly print in that streak, and the 4-week average decelerated to +4.37%. That divergence between the durable trend and the recent spot softness is worth watching rather than alarming; seasonal patterns typically compress pending counts through late summer before a modest September uptick. Inventory on the Altos side reached roughly 1.075 million active listings, up about 1.2% YoY, with new listings running just +0.4% YoY on the 4-week average — a sharp deceleration from earlier this year when supply was growing faster. Pending days on market sat at 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, confirming homes are taking longer to go under contract even as overall supply remains constrained. The NAR July existing home sales print came in at 4.06 million SAAR, beating expectations by roughly 210,000 units, though it ticked down from June and YoY growth of +1.25% is modest. More notable: NAR's inventory reading flipped negative YoY (-0.65%), the first such reading in some time, while new home sales for June landed at 628,000 SAAR, down 5.1% YoY, with builders cutting median prices nearly 2.7% YoY — a sign they are actively managing pace over margin. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.75% as of August 18, essentially flat on the month. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.01 percentage points — still well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points, meaning a meaningful portion of today's rate is lender risk premium rather than underlying benchmark costs. That spread has been compressing, down 24 basis points YoY, and if it continues toward historical norms, rates could ease 20-30 basis points without any Fed action — the affordability story most buyers aren't hearing. Against that backdrop, the MBA purchase index 4-week average is now -1.2% YoY after several months of positive readings, a reversal worth flagging. The Xactus MII, which typically leads MBA by 2-4 weeks, ticked up at the end of June (4-week average +2.31%) before MBA turned negative — the two are now diverging, and the MII's 13-week average is still negative YoY (-2.38%), suggesting the MBA softness may have legs into September. July housing starts were the headline to digest: 1.239 million SAAR, down 12.4% from June and 12.8% YoY, badly missing the 1.35 million consensus. Single-family starts fell nearly 10% on the month. Permits, however, told a different story — 1.443 million SAAR, up 6% YoY and the highest since January 2024, with single-family permits at their best since May 2024. Builders are authorizing future supply while completing their existing pipeline at a slower pace, a starts-permits divergence that implies new inventory pressure will ease through late 2026 even as the permit data reassures on 2027 supply.

The macro backdrop shifted subtly but meaningfully this month. July payrolls came in well below consensus — the actual change missed by roughly 80,000 jobs — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% against a 4.4% expectation, and the long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) actually declined for a second consecutive month. That combination matters for housing: a dropping headline rate sustains buyer confidence, while the receding long-term unemployment cohort reduces the near-term delinquency pipeline that had been building earlier this year. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1% in July against a 0.3% consensus, and core PCE came in at +0.13% for June against a 0.3% consensus — both of which open room for the Fed to move without being forced to. Core CPI for July printed +2.47% YoY, and shelter CPI, which the Fed watches as a lagging confirmation of rent trends, rose +3.16% YoY — still elevated but the real-time Zillow rent index shows rents up only 3.0% YoY and the ZORI ticked down in July, so shelter CPI will continue drifting lower over the next several months as older leases reprice. The 10-year Treasury yield firmed to 4.74%, which is the binding constraint on the 30-year rate right now; until that yield comes in, spread compression alone cannot push the 30-year below roughly 6.45-6.50% even in a favorable scenario.
