**August 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through most of the summer but is showing its first real cracks. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has run positive year-over-year for 55 consecutive weeks, and the 4-week average was still up 4.4% YoY as of late June — but the most recent weekly read turned negative at -3.0% YoY, the first negative week in that streak, and pending days on market have stretched to nearly 71 days, up 6.7% year-over-year. That combination — slowing weekly demand plus longer time to contract — is worth watching carefully. Inventory growth, which had been one of the cleaner stories of 2026, is also decelerating: total active supply is up just over 1% YoY on the Altos weekly read, and new listings flipped negative YoY in late June for the first time in months, with the 4-week average barely holding positive at +0.4%. NAR's July existing home sales came in at 4.06 million SAAR, beating expectations of 3.85 million and running +1.25% YoY — a solid headline — but the NSA read tells the more honest story: +2.8% YoY, with the Northeast the only region posting consistent volume gains. NAR's inventory figure actually contracted to 1.54 million units, flipping negative YoY for the first time after months of growth, which tightens the supply picture even as demand softens. The bigger divergence is in new homes: July new home sales fell to 607,000 SAAR, down 10.5% on the month and 5.0% YoY — while months supply for new homes jumped to 9.6, the highest in over a year. Builders are sitting on inventory they can't clear, and July starts fell sharply to 1,239,000 SAAR, missing expectations by 111,000 units and down 12.75% YoY; single-family starts dropped 15% YoY. The one offset: total permits rose 5.2% YoY and beat expectations, with single-family permits at their highest since May 2024 — suggesting builders are positioning for a future selling environment they expect to improve, even as they slow current production. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.75% today, essentially flat over the past month and up 23 basis points year-over-year. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.10 percentage points — well above the historical norm near 1.70 — and has ticked back up slightly after several weeks of mild compression. If spreads retrace toward 1.85–1.90, rates fall meaningfully without any Fed action; that remains the most underappreciated affordability lever in this market. MBA purchase applications are the cautionary note: the 4-week average is down 3.3% YoY and has declined for two consecutive weeks, with the 13-week average barely clinging to flat (+0.02% YoY). The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks, bounced in late June after a seven-week decline in its 13-week average — but both the spot MII and 4-week average remain negative YoY at -5.6% and -2.3% respectively. That MII-to-MBA divergence has not yet resolved cleanly, which means the September purchase application picture is genuinely uncertain. Watch the weekly Altos pending count and MBA applications closely over the next four weeks for confirmation of whether the late-June demand softness was seasonal noise or the beginning of a broader pullback.

The macro backdrop is sending competing signals, and the housing-relevant read is more nuanced than the headlines suggest. July payrolls missed badly — the economy added roughly 23,000 jobs against a consensus of 59,000 — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, beating expectations of 4.4%, and initial jobless claims printed at 206,000, well below consensus and the lowest in nearly a year. The long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) actually declined by 8.6% on the month to 1.771 million, reversing several months of accumulation. That is genuinely reassuring: the cohort most likely to produce future mortgage distress is shrinking, not growing. The mechanism matters here — long-term unemployed borrowers are the leading edge of delinquency pressure 6–12 months out, and their decline removes one of the more persistent tail risks. On inflation, both headline and core CPI came in cooler than expected in July, with core CPI at 2.47% YoY; however, Core PCE for July printed at 3.34% YoY, the Fed's preferred gauge, and that is still running comfortably above target. Shelter CPI held at +3.16% YoY — decelerating but sticky, and it will take several more months of observed rent softening (ZORI was up 3.0% YoY in July but down on the month) to move it meaningfully lower.
