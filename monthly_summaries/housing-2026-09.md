# Monthly Housing Summary — September 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The four-week read on housing demand has softened noticeably heading into September. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales remained positive year-over-year for the 55th consecutive week, but the momentum has slowed: the 4-week average YoY slipped to +4.4% from levels above +6% earlier in the summer, and the most recent weekly print turned negative at -3.0% YoY — the first negative weekly reading in over a year. That single-week reversal deserves watching but not overreading; the 13-week trend at +4.8% YoY still describes a market that is transacting. Active inventory on the Altos count stood just above 1.07 million, rising for eight consecutive weeks and up roughly 1.2% YoY, a deceleration from the double-digit inventory growth we saw through much of 2024 and early 2025. New listings turned negative YoY at -7.1% on the most recent weekly read, though the 4-week average remains barely positive at +0.4%, suggesting sellers are not flooding the market and the inventory build is gradual. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — a modest but persistent signal that absorption is slower than a year ago. NAR's August existing home sales printed at 3.98 million SAAR, beating the consensus of 3.85 million but still down 0.5% YoY and the third consecutive monthly decline — a result worth contextualizing: July was revised in a way that flatters August by comparison, and the NSA YoY tells the cleaner story at -3.5%. New home sales in July fell to 607K SAAR, down 5% YoY with months of supply jumping to 9.6, a level that historically compels builder price concessions. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.07% as of today, up roughly 10 basis points over the past month and 78 basis points higher than a year ago, with the 10-year Treasury at 4.92%. The mortgage-Treasury spread — currently 2.15 percentage points, versus a historical norm near 1.7 — has actually widened slightly over the past two weeks after compressing earlier this summer, which means the affordability relief that spread compression could deliver has stalled. MBA purchase applications ran -4.2% YoY on the 4-week average through September 4, with the 13-week average now down nine consecutive weeks; the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index, which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks, was showing a tentative recovery off its lows in late June, so the next few weeks of MBA data will clarify whether that early signal had legs or was noise. On the supply side, July housing starts missed badly at 1,239K versus 1,350K expected, down 12.8% YoY, with single-family starts off 15% YoY — the cleanest sign yet that builders are pulling back meaningfully. What partially offsets that: single-family permits at 894K are at their highest since May 2024 and up 2.2% YoY, suggesting the pullback in starts may reflect weather, financing, and lot-selection timing rather than a full strategic retreat. Watch whether October permit data confirms or reverses that.

The macro backdrop is genuinely mixed in ways that matter for housing. August payrolls came in at +162,000 — well above the +59,000 consensus — and the unemployment rate held at 4.1%, which is a labor market that still supports mortgage qualification and household formation. But dig one level deeper and the JOLTS data from July tells a more cautious story: the hires rate fell to 3.2% and quits fell to 3.056 million, both reversing recent gains. A declining quits rate means workers are less confident about finding a new job, which tends to suppress the life-event moves — divorce, job change, relocation — that drive a meaningful share of housing transactions. The more important signal to watch is the long-term unemployed cohort: workers unemployed 27 weeks or longer jumped 9% in a single month to 1.93 million, and median unemployment duration rose to 11.4 weeks, up 15% YoY. This does not threaten the current selling season, but it is accumulating in a way that will show up in delinquency data in the first half of 2027 if it persists. On inflation, core PCE ran at 3.34% YoY through July — still above the Fed's target — while core CPI came in at 2.47% YoY, cooler than expected, and shelter CPI decelerated to +3.16% YoY, continuing its gradual descent from the peaks. The divergence between core PCE and core CPI is partly mechanical (shelter weight and imputation differ), but as long as core PCE stays above 3%, the Fed has limited room to ease, which keeps the 10-year anchored and mortgage rates with it.
