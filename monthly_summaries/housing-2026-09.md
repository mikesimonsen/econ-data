**September 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive over the summer but is now showing real cracks at the leading edge. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales stood at roughly +4.8% YoY through late June and had run positive for 55 consecutive weeks — a streak that reflects the rate-driven demand recovery that began in late 2024. The 4-week average was still up 4.4% YoY at that same read, though the most recent weekly print turned negative (-3.0% YoY), the first negative weekly reading in over a year. Watch that weekly number: one negative week breaks no streaks, but two or three would. Inventory continues to rebuild — active listings on Realtor.com rose 3.8% YoY in August and Zillow's for-sale count was up 1.9% — while new listings are essentially flat to slightly negative YoY across platforms, meaning supply growth is decelerating rather than accelerating. Pending days on market at 70.9 days are running 6.7% above year-ago, a quiet signal that homes are sitting longer. NAR's August existing sales came in at 3.98 million SAAR, beating the 3.85 million consensus but still down 0.5% YoY in seasonally adjusted terms — and the NSA count was -3.5% YoY, with all four regions negative. New home sales told a sharper story: July printed 607K SAAR, down 10.5% from June and -5.0% YoY, with months supply jumping to 9.6 — builders are carrying more finished inventory than they have in years. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.20% today, up 83 basis points year-over-year, and the spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.20 points — still roughly 50 basis points above the long-run norm of about 1.70 points. That spread has ticked down 4 basis points in the most recent session and the 4-week average is running around 2.14, so there is modest compression underway; if it normalizes even partially, rates could fall meaningfully without any Fed action. The leading indicators for October are not encouraging: MBA purchase applications are down 10.2% YoY on the most recent weekly read, and the 4-week average is -6.0% YoY — the weakest since September 2024. The Xactus MII, which leads MBA by 2-4 weeks, was still showing a short-term bounce in late June (+3.5% on the week) but its 13-week trend was falling and its YoY was -5.6%, suggesting the MBA weakness visible now was telegraphed a month ago. What to watch over the next four weeks: whether the Altos weekly pending streak breaks, and whether the MII's late-June bounce translated into any MBA stabilization in July-August data yet to be released.

The macro backdrop this month is one of genuine tension: the labor market headline looks fine, but the internals are softening in ways that feed directly into housing confidence. August payrolls came in at 162K, well above the 59K consensus, and the unemployment rate held at 4.1% — both readings that, in isolation, suggest a healthy buyer pool. But the JOLTS hires rate fell to 3.2% in July (down 0.2 points on the month), quits dropped to a 1.9% rate, and — most relevant for the longer housing cycle — the number of workers unemployed 27 weeks or more rose 9.0% in a single month to 1.93 million, with median unemployment duration jumping to 11.4 weeks, up 15% year-over-year. Long-term unemployed workers don't affect this autumn's selling season, but they represent the cohort most likely to miss mortgage payments 6-12 months from now; watch this number accumulate. On inflation, August CPI came in at +3.35% YoY with core at +2.45%, and shelter CPI has firmed to +3.0% YoY — still decelerating from its peak but sticky enough to keep the Fed anchored. Core PCE through July was +3.34% YoY, above the Fed's 2% target, which is why the 10-year Treasury is sitting at 5.0% today and has little obvious reason to fall near-term. That 5-handle on the 10-year is the ceiling on affordability right now: with the 30-year fixed at 7.20%, monthly payments on a median-priced existing home have risen materially from a year ago, and the Case-Shiller data through June confirms the geography of that pressure. Chicago is up 6.9% YoY, New York 4.5%, Cleveland 4.1%, Boston 2.7% — all markets with constrained supply and employed buyers who haven't fled. Dallas is -0.6%, Phoenix -0.7%, Las Vegas -1.8%, Seattle -1.8%, Tampa -1.5% — Sun Belt markets where inventory rebuilt faster than demand could absorb it and where new construction added more competition.
