**September 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through the summer but is showing early signs of fatigue. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks — a durable streak — and the 4-week average stood at +4.4% YoY as of late June. The spot week turned negative (-3.0% YoY) for the first time in that run, which is worth noting without overreading: late-June is seasonally soft, and the 4-week and 13-week reads are still constructive. Inventory at roughly 1.07 million active listings was up just 1.2% YoY as of late June — inventory growth is clearly decelerating — and the 4-week new listings average came in nearly flat YoY (+0.4%) after several months of mild expansion. The pipeline of future supply is thinning. Pending days on market extended to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, confirming that homes are taking longer to go under contract even as supply stays constrained — a tell that buyer hesitancy, not seller scarcity, is the friction. July existing home sales printed at 4.06 million SAAR, beating consensus of 3.85 million and running +1.3% YoY, though the headline slightly overstates the strength: the NSA count was only +2.8% YoY, and the sequential dip from June reflects genuine softening in the South and West. The more troubling divergence is new home sales, which fell to 607,000 SAAR in July — down 10.5% from June and -5.0% YoY — while new home months of supply jumped to 9.6, the highest in over a year. Builders responded: July starts missed badly at 1,239,000 (expected 1,350,000), down 12.7% YoY, with single-family starts off 15% YoY and South starts at their weakest since April 2023. Permits, by contrast, rose — total permits beat consensus at 1,433,000, up 5.2% YoY, and single-family permits hit their highest since May 2024. That starts-permits divergence is the key builder signal: they are pulling future authorizations while working down existing backlogs, not launching new ones. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.89% today, up 36 basis points YoY, and the spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.10 points — still elevated relative to the long-run historical norm of roughly 1.70 points. The spread has been compressing slowly (down 21 basis points YoY on the 4-week average), and that compression alone — without any Fed action — is the mechanism through which rates could fall meaningfully if credit risk appetite improves. MBA purchase applications (4-week average) were -3.3% YoY as of August 21, and the 13-week average has flipped to barely positive (+0.02% YoY) after 80 weeks in the green, a sign the demand pipeline is narrowing. The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by 2-4 weeks, had ticked up in late June — a modest positive divergence from MBA's softness — but the MII 13-week average was still -2.4% YoY and falling for seven straight weeks. Watch whether the MII bounce in late June translates into MBA stabilization in September; if it does not follow through, the demand read for the fall season deteriorates meaningfully.

The macro backdrop is genuinely mixed in ways that matter differently for housing. July payrolls missed badly — actual +23,000 against a consensus expectation of +59,000 — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, beating the 4.4% consensus, and initial claims have been running well below year-ago levels (203,000 last week, -11.4% YoY). The JOLTS data released today complicates the picture: the hires rate slipped back to 3.2% and the quits rate to 1.9%, both below year-ago levels, which signals a labor market where employed workers feel less confident about their next move — the same psychological condition that suppresses voluntary relocations and, by extension, listing supply. That mechanism — not recession fear, but labor market caution — is why new listings have stalled. On inflation: core CPI came in at +2.5% YoY in July, cooler than expected, and core PCE at +3.3% YoY is still running above the Fed's 2% target but decelerating at the margin.
