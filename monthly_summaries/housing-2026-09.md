**September 2026 Housing Summary**

Weekly demand held positive through most of the summer, but the most recent Altos read — week ending June 26 — shows new pending sales at 85,813, down 3.0% year-over-year, snapping what had been a 55-week streak of positive YoY readings. That single-week dip deserves context: the four-week average remains up 4.4% YoY, and the 13-week average, which has been rising for 14 consecutive weeks, sits at +4.8% YoY. One week of negative readings against a still-positive rolling trend is noise until it isn't — watch whether the four-week average follows the spot reading lower over the next two to three weeks. Inventory continued its gradual climb, with Altos total supply rising for eight straight weeks and sitting 1.2% above year-ago levels, while new listings on a four-week basis were essentially flat YoY (+0.4%) after a brief negative dip. Pending days on market came in at 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — homes are taking longer to go under contract than a year ago, which is consistent with buyers having slightly more leverage at the margin. NAR's August existing home sales print came in at 3.98 million SAAR, down 0.5% YoY — technically negative, but it beat expectations by a meaningful 130,000 units. On the NSA basis (the truer read for direction), August sales ran 3.5% below last August. New home sales told a harder story: the July print fell 10.5% month-over-month to 607,000 SAAR, down 5.0% YoY, with new-home months supply jumping to 9.6 — elevated enough to keep builder incentive pressure alive. The 30-year fixed rate sat at 7.19% as of September 21, essentially unchanged on the month after a brief uptick — and the spread to the 10-year Treasury has widened back to 2.23 percentage points, moving away from the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points. That spread widening is the affordability story few buyers are hearing: even without a Fed move, if the spread reverts toward 1.9–2.0 points — as it briefly did earlier this year — the 30-year rate would fall to roughly the low-to-mid 6s on the same Treasury yield. MBA purchase applications are running 6.0% below year-ago levels on a four-week average, the weakest reading since September 2024, and the 13-week average has fallen for 10 consecutive weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks — showed a bounce in late June (four-week average up 2.3%), suggesting the MBA deterioration may decelerate in coming weeks, though the MII's 13-week trend is still falling. August single-family starts firmed to 918,000 SAAR (+5.6% YoY), a notable reversal, but total starts missed at 1,275,000 driven by a sharp drop in multifamily. Single-family permits eased slightly to 878,000 but remain 2.3% above year-ago levels and at their highest since May 2024 — builders are still authorizing single-family supply, just more cautiously. Watch whether the MII bounce translates into a stabilization of the MBA four-week average through early October; that's the clearest near-term signal on fall demand.

The macro backdrop going into the fourth quarter is genuinely mixed in ways that matter for housing. August payrolls came in at 162,000 — well above the 59,000 consensus — and the unemployment rate held at 4.1%, also better than the 4.4% expected. On the surface, that looks supportive: employed people buy houses. The complication is what's happening beneath the headline. The hires rate fell back to 3.2% in July and quits fell to 1.9%, both reversing recent improvements — labor market fluidity, which drives the move-up buyer, is contracting. More concerning for the medium term: the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) rose 9.0% in a single month to 1.93 million, and median unemployment duration jumped to 11.4 weeks, up 15% YoY. Neither of these is a 2026 selling-season problem, but they are the leading edge of future mortgage stress if the duration trend continues. On inflation, August headline CPI rose 3.35% YoY and core CPI came in at 2.45% — both cooler than expected, with shelter CPI moderating to +3.0% YoY. Core PCE through July was running at 3.34% YoY, still above the Fed's target, which is why the 10-year Treasury has drifted back toward 4.95% and is keeping the 30-year mortgage anchored above 7%. The spread widening noted above — from roughly 2.0 points earlier this summer back to 2.23 — suggests lenders are adding a modest risk premium even as the Treasury yield has been range-bound.
