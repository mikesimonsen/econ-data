**September 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive through summer but is showing clear deceleration. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales was running at +4.81% YoY as of the week ending June 26 — the 55th consecutive week of positive year-over-year pending sales — though the spot week dipped to -2.99% YoY, snapping a streak that had run positive for most of the prior year. The 4-week average at +4.37% still shows net buyers in the market, but the trend line is flattening. Inventory at roughly 1.07 million active listings rose 1.17% YoY on the Altos read, a modest gain still well below pre-pandemic norms; new listings ticked down 7% YoY on the spot week, though the 4-week average edged just barely positive, suggesting sellers haven't retreated but aren't pressing. Pending days on market at 70.9 days sits 6.7% above last year — transactions are taking longer to close, which is worth flagging at listing appointments when sellers ask about timing. The NAR existing sales print for July came in at 4.06 million SAAR, beating the 3.85 million consensus by a meaningful margin and up 1.25% YoY, though it fell slightly from June — treat that sequential dip as seasonal noise against a positive YoY backdrop. New home sales told a different story: 607,000 SAAR in July, down 10.5% from June and off 5% YoY, with months supply at 9.6 — builders have inventory to move and have been cutting median prices (-0.88% YoY). On rates, the 30-year fixed sits at 6.91% as of September 2, up about 40 basis points YoY. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.12 percentage points — historically that spread runs near 1.7 points, so there is roughly 40 basis points of compression potential if credit and volatility conditions normalize without the Fed moving at all. That is the affordability lever most buyers aren't seeing. The MBA purchase index 4-week average came in at -2.81% YoY, a mild negative but reversing slightly off its recent low; critically, the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA applications by two to four weeks by measuring credit-pull intent earlier in the funnel — was down 5.6% YoY on its spot week and -2.3% on the 4-week average as of late June. The MII's weakness preceded the MBA softness, and both are now pointing in the same direction. What to watch in the next four weeks: whether the Altos 13-week pending average holds above +4% YoY as fall seasonality kicks in, and whether the building permits rebound (+5.2% YoY in July, with single-family permits at their highest since May 2024) translates into a stabilization of starts — which fell 12.75% YoY in July, missing expectations badly and flagging a meaningful pullback in future supply that won't be felt in inventory until 2027-2028.

The macro backdrop is legitimately two-sided. July payrolls missed badly — the economy added roughly 23,000 net jobs against a 59,000 consensus, though the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, beating the 4.4% expectation, which complicates the read. The mechanism for housing: a soft jobs print nudges rate expectations lower, which is constructive for mortgage rates, but the JOLTS data released alongside showed the hires rate falling to 3.2% and quits to 1.9% — both declining — which describes a labor market where workers feel less mobile and less confident about voluntary job changes. That hesitancy suppresses the life-event moves (job change → relocation → home purchase) that drive transaction volume independently of rate levels. On inflation, Core PCE came in at +3.34% YoY for July — running above the Fed's 2% target and edging higher for seven consecutive months — while headline CPI at +3.3% YoY printed cooler than the 0.9% MoM consensus expected. The shelter component of CPI rose +3.16% YoY and continues to lag the real-time rent data; Zillow's ZORI showed rents up only 3.0% YoY in July with a slight monthly dip, suggesting CPI shelter will decelerate further over the next few months. That matters because shelter's weight in Core PCE is large enough that its deceleration could push the headline closer to target even without broader disinflation — giving the Fed more room to move. The 10-year Treasury sits at 4.79%, up 51 basis points YoY; with Core PCE at 3.34%, the real yield is about 145 basis points, which is historically not extreme, but the absolute rate level keeps affordability constrained. On regional price trends, the June Case-Shiller data (released August 27, referencing June closings) shows the two-speed market sharpening: Chicago is up 6.94% YoY and New York up 4.49%, both rising for seven consecutive months
