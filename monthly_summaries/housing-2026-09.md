**September 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held its footing through the summer but is showing early signs of fatigue at the edges. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks, running at roughly +4.8% year-over-year as of late June — a durable streak — but the most recent weekly print slipped to -3.0% YoY, and the 4-week average decelerated to +4.4%, suggesting the streak's momentum is softening rather than reversing. Watch that 13-week trend closely; if it crosses below +3%, the headline narrative changes. Inventory at Altos stood just above 1.07 million active listings as of late June, up about 1.2% YoY — growth that has been decelerating steadily, and by August, Realtor.com's active count confirmed the same story: +3.8% YoY but with new listings essentially flat (-0.1% YoY) and pending listings ticking into negative YoY territory (-0.2%). Pending days on market have been elevated for 103 consecutive weeks, and at 70.9 days they remain about 6.7% above year-ago levels — that's not alarming, but it means buyers are not rushing. NAR existing home sales came in at 4.06 million SAAR for July, +1.25% YoY on the NSA basis and a beat versus the 3.85 million consensus — but the SAR trend is now two months lower, and inventory slipped back YoY after briefly turning positive. The divergence with new home sales is worth flagging: new homes sold at 607,000 SAAR in July, down 10.5% from June and -5.0% YoY, with months supply jumping to 9.6 — builders are sitting on more unsold product even as they pulled starts back sharply (-12.4% in July, -12.8% YoY, a meaningful miss vs. the 1,350K consensus). Permits told a different story: total permits rose to 1,433K, beating expectations, with single-family permits at their highest since May 2024. The starts-vs.-permits gap tells you builders are authorized but not breaking ground — they're watching cancellations and mortgage rates before committing. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.88% as of September 3, essentially flat over the past month, and 39 basis points above a year ago. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.11-2.12 points — still roughly 40-45 basis points wide of the historical norm near 1.70. That excess spread is the affordability story hiding in plain sight: the Fed doesn't need to move for rates to fall meaningfully if lenders regain confidence and the spread compresses. MBA purchase applications ticked up in the latest week but the 13-week average is down -0.5% YoY and has been falling for eight consecutive weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA by roughly 2-4 weeks — bounced off its recent lows in late June (4-week average +2.3%), but its 13-week average was still down -2.4% YoY and falling for seven weeks. The MII bounce did not yet confirm in MBA; watch whether that transmission shows up in September application data.

The macro backdrop this month was genuinely better than expected on the surface, but the details deserve a closer read. August payrolls printed +162,000 against a consensus of +59,000, and the unemployment rate held at 4.1% versus the 4.4% expected — two clean beats. Wages rose 3.1% YoY, which is positive for purchasing power but also keeps the Fed cautious on rate cuts, particularly with Core PCE running at 3.34% YoY through July. That's the tension: labor looks fine by the headline numbers, but the hires rate fell to 3.2% in July (down 0.1 point YoY) and the quits rate slipped to 1.9%, both signaling a labor market where workers feel less mobile and employers are filling fewer positions. Less mobility means fewer life-event-driven moves — job relocations, voluntary upgrades — which is a quiet drag on transaction volume that doesn't show up in the unemployment rate. The signal worth watching most carefully is the long-term unemployed cohort: workers unemployed 27 weeks or more rose to 1.93 million in August, up nearly 9% in a single month, and median unemployment duration jumped to 11.4 weeks, +15% YoY. This cohort does not affect the current selling season, but it accumulates into future mortgage stress — watch it over the next two to three quarters. On inflation, shelter CPI ticked up just 0.14% in July (+3.16% YoY), continuing its slow descent toward the Zillow and Apartment List real-time rent measures that show rents running closer to +3.0%. If shelter CPI continues decelerating, it removes one of the Fed's remaining arguments for holding rates elevated, which would eventually ease pressure on the 10-year and, through spread compression, on the 30-year.
