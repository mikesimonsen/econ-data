# Monthly Housing Summary — September 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The four weeks ending September 15 showed demand softening at the margin while the rate environment remained a meaningful headwind. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales held positive year-over-year — that streak reached 55 consecutive weeks as of the most recent read — but the spot week turned negative at -3.0% YoY and the 4-week average, while still up 4.4% YoY, has been decelerating. That deceleration matters more than the headline streak right now. Inventory on the Altos side continued to build, with total supply up roughly 1.2% YoY for eight straight weeks, though new listings flipped negative YoY (-7.1% on the spot week), suggesting sellers are pulling back even as the existing stack grows — a pattern consistent with a market where buyers are hesitating and owners who do not have to move are waiting. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, a signal that matches the deceleration in contract activity. The NAR existing home sales print for August came in at 3.98 million SAAR — a beat against the 3.85 million consensus but still down 0.5% YoY, the first negative YoY SAAR reading after several months of positive prints. The NSA figure tells a starker story: 363,000 units, down 3.5% YoY. New home sales fell harder in July — 607,000 SAAR, -5.0% YoY — with months supply on new homes at 9.6, a level that reflects builders sitting on completed inventory rather than moving it. The 30-year fixed rate is at 7.22% as of today, up roughly a full point YoY and rising for five consecutive days. The 10-year Treasury is at 5.01%, and the 30Y-10Y spread sits at approximately 2.21 points on a daily basis — well above the historical norm of around 1.7 points, meaning roughly half a percentage point of borrowing cost above what the Treasury market alone would imply. The weekly average spread has been creeping up three straight weeks. That spread is the affordability story hiding in plain sight: if it compresses toward its long-run average, the 30-year rate falls meaningfully without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications are running -4.2% YoY on the 4-week average and the 13-week average has fallen nine consecutive weeks, the latter an unusual enough drop to register statistically. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which leads MBA applications by roughly two to four weeks — showed a spot uptick in late June after seven weeks of decline in its 13-week average, but the 4-week MII YoY remains -2.3%, not yet confirming a turn. The divergence between the MII bounce and the continued MBA deterioration is the leading-indicator tension to watch over the next four weeks. On the builder side, the starts-permits divergence is notable: July single-family permits rose to their highest since May 2024, up 2.2% YoY, while single-family starts fell 15% YoY and total starts missed consensus by roughly 110,000 units. Builders are greenlighting future supply while clearing less of the current pipeline — a combination that will matter for 2027 inventory but not for this selling season.

On the macro side, three data points stand out for their housing relevance. First, August payrolls came in at roughly 162,000 — a significant beat against the 59,000 consensus — and the unemployment rate held at 4.1% against a 4.4% expectation. Solid employment is what keeps existing homeowners current and buyers willing to commit; the beat removes one near-term downside risk to transaction volume. Second, August CPI came in at +0.40% month-over-month, well below the 0.9% consensus, with core CPI at +2.45% YoY and shelter CPI decelerating to +3.0% YoY — the latter continuing its slow grind lower, which is the mechanism through which Fed rate expectations will eventually ease and pull the 10-year down with them. Core PCE for July printed at +3.34% YoY, still above the Fed's 2% target, so the path from "inflation cooling" to "rate relief" remains measured. Third, JOLTS data for July showed hires falling to a 3.2% rate and quits to 1.9% — both below year-ago levels — which points to a labor market where workers feel less mobile. Reduced labor mobility historically correlates with reduced housing mobility; people who feel less confident about finding a new job are less likely to take on a new mortgage. The long-term unemployed cohort (27+ weeks) ticked up 9% in a single month to 1.93 million, reversing a recent decline. That number does not affect October closings, but it is worth tracking as a leading indicator of mortgage stress in the six-to-twelve month window.
