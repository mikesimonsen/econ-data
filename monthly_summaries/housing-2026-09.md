**September 2026 Housing Summary**

The most important housing demand signal this month is the deceleration in Altos new pending sales. The 13-week average has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks, but the spot week came in at -3.0% YoY as of June 26, and the 4-week average, while still positive at +4.4% YoY, has been decelerating. That streak remains intact for now, but it is narrowing. Inventory on the Altos side held around 1.07 million active listings, up about 1.2% YoY — modest growth that has been rising eight consecutive weeks but well below the pace we saw earlier in the year. New listings ticked down 7.1% YoY in the spot week, though the 4-week average was still marginally positive at +0.4% YoY, and the 13-week average has been climbing for 14 weeks. Pending days on market extended to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY — the 103rd consecutive week above year-ago levels — which tells you the pace of absorption has softened measurably. NAR's July existing home sales came in at 4.06 million SAAR, beating the consensus of 3.85 million but still down slightly from June on a seasonally adjusted basis and only +1.3% YoY — confirming that the market is moving but not accelerating. New home sales diverged notably: the July print was 607,000 SAAR, down 10.5% from June and off 5.0% YoY, while new home months supply jumped to 9.6 — a level that typically compels builders to pull pricing or incentives. On rates, the 30-year fixed sits at 6.89% as of September 4, up 44 basis points from a year ago and essentially flat over the past month. The more interesting figure is the spread to the 10-year Treasury, which is running at 2.11-2.13 basis points — still meaningfully elevated relative to the historical norm of around 1.7 points. If that spread compresses toward historical norms as volatility recedes, the 30-year could fall 40 basis points without the Fed moving at all; that is the affordability story most buyer conversations are missing. MBA purchase applications printed at 157.8 for the week of August 28, up 2.2% from the prior week but still -0.6% YoY, and the 13-week average has been falling for eight consecutive weeks, now slightly negative YoY. The Xactus MII — which tends to lead MBA purchase applications by two to four weeks by capturing mortgage intent earlier in the consumer decision cycle — came in at 123.3 on its spot reading, up 3.5% from the prior week but -5.6% YoY, and its 13-week average has been falling for seven weeks. The MII and MBA are telling the same story right now: demand is not collapsing, but it is not firming either. The signal to watch over the next four weeks is whether the September Altos 4-week pending average holds above zero YoY or rolls through it — that will determine whether the 55-week streak ends heading into the fall selling season. Builder permits provided a more constructive read: total permits rose to 1.433 million SAAR in July, beating expectations and reversing two months of declines, with single-family permits at their highest since May 2024. Starts, however, fell sharply to 1.239 million SAAR, missing by 111,000 and down 12.7% YoY. The permits-over-starts gap suggests builders are authorizing future supply while deliberately slowing their current production pace — relevant for inventory in 2027, not this fall.

On the macro side, three data points matter most for housing right now. First, the August payroll report was genuinely strong: 162,000 jobs added against a consensus of 59,000, unemployment held at 4.1% against an expectation of 4.4%, and average hourly earnings rose 3.1% YoY. A labor market that is stronger than feared supports household formation and reduces the probability of forced sales — that is the direct housing link. The counterweight is that JOLTS hires fell 5.2% in July and the quits rate edged down, suggesting workers are less willing to change jobs, which historically correlates with reduced household mobility and fewer discretionary moves. Second, Core PCE for July came in at +3.34% YoY, above the Fed's 2% target and still rising on a seven-month trend. That, combined with a 10-year Treasury yield anchored at 4.76%, is what is keeping the 30-year rate close to 6.9%. If Core PCE does not decelerate meaningfully in the August print (due late September), the rate ceiling stays in place through at least Q4. Third, the long-term unemployment picture deserves attention even though it has no effect on this fall's sales pace: the number of workers unemployed 27 weeks or more rose 9% in August to 1.93 million, and the median unemployment duration jumped to 11.4 weeks — up 15% YoY
