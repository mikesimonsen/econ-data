# Monthly Housing Summary — September 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The Altos 13-week pending sales average held positive year-over-year for the 55th consecutive week through late June, but the more recent data tells a different story: the spot weekly reading turned negative YoY as of June 26, and the 4-week average, while still up 4.4% YoY, decelerated noticeably. That divergence between a still-positive rolling trend and a weakening weekly signal deserves attention as we enter October. Inventory continued its slow climb — active listings on Realtor.com rose 3.8% YoY in August, Zillow's for-sale count was up 1.9% — but new listings are contracting: Altos new listings turned negative YoY in late June, and the Realtor.com new listing count was essentially flat YoY in August (-0.1%). Less fresh supply hitting the market even as total inventory grows means the homes sitting on market are sitting longer; Realtor.com median days on market ticked up to 60, flat YoY but rising for three consecutive months. NAR existing home sales came in at 3.98 million SAAR for August — above the 3.85 million consensus, but down 0.5% YoY, and the NSA read was off 3.5% YoY, a meaningful step back after a brief positive stretch. New home sales (July, the most recent print) fell 10.5% month-over-month and 5.0% YoY to 607,000 SAAR, with months supply jumping to 9.6 — elevated enough that builders are sitting on finished inventory. The 30-year fixed rate stands at 7.17% as of September 22, up 82 basis points year-over-year and essentially unmoved over the past month. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.22 percentage points — roughly 50 basis points above the long-run norm of about 1.70 — and has been drifting wider over the past four weeks rather than compressing. That spread is the affordability story nobody is telling: if it normalized even partially, to 1.90–2.00 points, the 30-year would be in the low-to-mid 6s without any Fed action. Instead, with the 10-year at 5.12% and rising, rates are being pushed in the wrong direction for buyers. MBA purchase applications offer little comfort: the 4-week average is down 7.4% YoY as of September 18 — its weakest reading since August 2024 — and the 13-week average has fallen for 11 consecutive weeks. The Xactus MII (a credit-inquiry-based leading indicator that tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks) was still showing a slight uptick in late June, but the 13-week MII average had been falling for seven consecutive weeks at that point; the subsequent MBA deterioration is consistent with what the MII was telegraphing. On the supply side, single-family starts recovered in August to 918,000 SAAR (+7.6% MoM), but completions fell sharply — total completions hit their lowest YoY pace since June 2011, down 27.5% — meaning the pipeline of near-term deliveries is thinning even as permits remain modestly positive YoY. Watch whether the 10-year stabilizes or continues its recent move higher; that, more than any Fed decision, will determine where purchase activity goes in October.

The macro backdrop explains much of the housing slowdown, and the pieces fit together in a specific way. August payrolls printed 162,000 — well above the 59,000 consensus — and unemployment held at 4.1%, below the 4.4% most forecasters expected. On the surface that looks supportive of housing, but the JOLTS data from July complicates the picture: the hires rate fell to 3.2% and the quits rate dropped to 1.9%, both below year-ago levels. When people stop voluntarily leaving jobs, geographic mobility slows, and the move-up and relocation transactions that drive existing-home volume contract. More telling is what is happening inside the unemployment numbers: the count of workers unemployed 27 or more weeks rose 9% in a single month to 1.93 million, and median unemployment duration jumped to 11.4 weeks — up 15% year-over-year. The headline rate looks fine; the duration data says the labor market is bifurcating, with a growing cohort of workers who cannot find re-employment quickly. That cohort is not a current-selling-season problem, but it is the forward delinquency story to track over the next six to twelve months. On inflation: Core PCE was 3.34% YoY through July, and CPI shelter ran at 3.0% YoY in August — still sticky, but decelerating. The shelter component matters here because it keeps measured inflation elevated even as actual market rents (Zillow ZORI: +2.2% YoY) are running well below it; as that gap closes over the next several months, core readings should drift lower, which is the path to any Fed easing.
