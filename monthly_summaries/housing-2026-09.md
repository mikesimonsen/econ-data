**September 2026 Housing Summary**

Weekly demand held positive through the first half of the summer but softened into late June. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales was running +4.81% YoY through the week of June 26 — a streak of 55 consecutive weeks of positive YoY pending sales on that smoothed basis — though the spot week turned negative (-2.99%) and the 4-week average decelerated to +4.37%. That deceleration matters more than the spot dip; 13-week momentum has been the reliable signal all year, and it was still rising as recently as mid-June. Inventory continued to build, with total active supply up roughly 1-5% YoY depending on the source (Altos near +1%, Realtor.com at +3.76%, Zillow at +1.89%), though new listings growth has stalled — the Altos 4-week average turned flat YoY in late June after several weeks of modest gains, and both Redfin and Realtor.com showed new listing counts drifting negative YoY into August. Pending days on market (Altos) sat at 70.9, up 6.7% YoY, confirming that the market is absorbing supply at a slower pace than a year ago but without the acute freeze of 2023. NAR existing home sales printed 3.98 million SAAR for August — actually 130,000 units above consensus, though still down 0.5% YoY and the lowest sequential print in three months; the NSA figure of 363,000 units was down 3.5% YoY. New home sales told a starker story: the July SAAR of 607,000 fell 5% YoY, months supply jumped to 9.6, and the builder pipeline is visibly stressing — total completions collapsed 27.5% YoY in August to their lowest level since June 2011, while single-family starts bounced back +7.6% in August after a weak July. Permit activity is worth watching: single-family permits at 878,000 are at their highest since May 2024, but they ticked down from July, and the starts-completions gap is wide enough that near-term resale competition from builder deliveries will be limited. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.17% today, up 82 basis points YoY and essentially unchanged over the past month. The mortgage-Treasury spread — the gap between the 30-year rate and the 10-year yield — is 2.22 percentage points, well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points; the 4-week average has been rising for two weeks. Until that spread compresses toward historical norms, rates cannot fall materially even if the 10-year holds here or drifts lower. MBA purchase applications averaged -5.97% YoY on the 4-week basis through September 11, the weakest reading since September 2024, and the 13-week average has fallen for 10 consecutive weeks. The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by 2-4 weeks, showed a brief uptick in late June but its 13-week average was still declining — that divergence between a short-term bounce and a deteriorating trend average is exactly the pattern worth monitoring heading into October. Watch whether MBA applications can stabilize above the -6% YoY level; a sustained break below it would signal that fall contract volume will disappoint.

The macro backdrop is the most consequential variable right now, and it is sending mixed signals. August payrolls came in at +162,000 — nearly triple the +59,000 consensus — and the unemployment rate held at 4.1%, also better than the 4.4% forecast. That is a labor market that, on the headline, does not scream recession. But the JOLTS data told a quieter story underneath: the hires rate fell to 3.2% in July, the quits rate dropped to 1.9%, and total quits reversed after a brief rise. A falling quits rate means fewer voluntary job changes, and voluntary job changes are one of the cleaner leading indicators of household formation decisions — people who feel confident about the labor market move. The long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) rose 9% in a single month to 1.93 million in August, and median unemployment duration climbed to 11.4 weeks, up 15% YoY. This cohort does not affect the current selling season directly, but it accumulates into future delinquency pressure if it persists; it is worth tracking. On inflation, August CPI came in cooler than feared at +3.35% YoY headline and +2.45% core, and shelter CPI held at +3.03% YoY — a continued slow deceleration from its 2023 peak that eventually feeds into Core PCE, which was still running +3.34% YoY through July. The Fed is not cutting into a 3.3% core PCE environment, and the 10-year at 4.95% reflects that reality.
