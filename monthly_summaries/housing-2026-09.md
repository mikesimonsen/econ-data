**September 2026 Housing Summary**

The clearest signal in the demand data is a softening trend beneath a still-positive headline. The Altos 13-week average for new pending sales sits at +4.81% year-over-year and has been positive for 55 consecutive weeks — a durable streak — but the 4-week average has decelerated to +4.37% YoY, and the spot week for June 26 ticked negative at -2.99% YoY for the first time in that streak. One negative spot week is not a trend, but the direction of travel matters. Inventory on the Altos side rose for eight consecutive weeks through late June, up just +1.17% YoY, while new listings softened — the 4-week average was barely positive at +0.41% YoY and the spot week turned negative at -7.14%, which pulled the 13-week average to +0.71% and decelerating. Pending days on market held at 70.9 days, up 6.71% YoY — still elevated, meaning the market is not clearing as briskly as a year ago. The NAR July existing home sales print came in at 4.06 million SAAR, up 1.25% YoY and well ahead of the 3.85M consensus, but note that the beat partly reflects a low bar; sales are still below any pre-pandemic July on record, and the SAAR dipped modestly from June. New home sales diverged sharply: 607K SAAR in July, down 10.5% from June and -5.0% YoY, with months supply jumping to 9.6 — that is builder-inventory territory that tends to produce further price concessions. Indeed, the new home median fell to $393,800, down 0.9% YoY, while starts collapsed 12.4% in July to 1,239K SAAR (-12.8% YoY), missing the 1,350K consensus badly. The one constructive supply-side signal: total permits rose to 1,433K SAAR, beating expectations, with single-family permits hitting their highest since May 2024 — builders are still authorizing future product even as they pull back on groundbreakings. The 30-year fixed sits at 6.88% as of September 3, down a few basis points over the past month but still 39bp above a year ago. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.11-2.12pp — roughly 40bp above the long-run norm of ~1.7pp. That gap is the hidden affordability story: if credit markets normalize without the Fed moving, rates fall toward 6.4-6.5% on spread compression alone. The MBA purchase index 4-week average is -2.81% YoY as of August 28, a mild negative, though it ticked up on the week. More concerning is the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — the 13-week average has been falling for seven weeks and sits at -2.38% YoY, running meaningfully below the MBA 4-week signal. Because the MII leads MBA by 2-4 weeks, that divergence suggests purchase application softness has further to run into September. Watch the September MBA 4-week average: if it moves below -4% YoY while the MII 4-week average (currently -2.30% YoY but rebounding) fails to confirm a turn, the fall selling season will open weaker than the July NAR beat implied.

The macro backdrop is legitimately two-sided. The August payroll print — +162K actual vs. +59K expected — was the largest positive surprise of the year, and the unemployment rate held at 4.1% against a 4.4% consensus, which removes the near-term recession narrative that had begun building. Hourly earnings rose 3.1% YoY, enough to slowly improve real purchasing power at current inflation. That combination is broadly constructive for housing demand: employed households with rising wages eventually transact. But two cracks are worth watching. JOLTS hires fell in July to 5,054K with the hires rate dropping 20bp to 3.2%, and quits fell as well — both signal a labor market where workers are less confident about job-switching, which historically precedes mobility-driven home purchases softening. More pointed: the long-term unemployed (27+ weeks) rose to 1.93 million in August, up nearly 9% in a single month, and median unemployment duration jumped to 11.4 weeks, up 15% YoY. This cohort does not affect the current selling season — these are not active buyers — but if it continues accumulating, it shapes the distressed-borrower pipeline 6-12 months out. On inflation, Core PCE came in at 3.34% YoY in July, still running above the Fed's target, while Core CPI at 2.47% YoY was below expectations and shelter CPI at 3.16% continued its slow deceleration — the lagged rental data is finally feeding through. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.76%, and with Core PCE still at 3.34%, real rates remain meaningfully restrictive; the Fed has little cover
