The Altos weekly demand read through June 26 shows new pending sales down 3.0% year-over-year on the spot read — the first negative week after a 55-week positive streak — though the 4-week average held at +4.4% YoY and the 13-week average remained at +4.8% YoY positive for 55 consecutive weeks, suggesting the single-week dip reflects seasonal timing more than a demand break. Inventory continued climbing, with Altos total active listings up 1.2% YoY and rising for eight consecutive weeks; new listings on the 13-week average were up 0.7% YoY and had improved for 14 straight weeks, though the spot week turned negative at -7.1% YoY — deceleration rather than reversal. Pending days on market ticked to 70.9 days, up 6.7% YoY, a modest but sustained widening that has now run 103 consecutive weeks. NAR existing home sales came in at 3.98 million SAAR for August, beating the 3.85 million consensus but still down 0.5% YoY on a seasonally adjusted basis; the NSA read was -3.5% YoY, and all four regions deteriorated on a YoY basis, with the West off 2.7% and the Midwest down 2.1%. New home sales for July fell to 607,000 SAAR, down 5.0% YoY, with months of supply at 9.6 — a level that explains why builders pulled back sharply: single-family starts dropped 15% YoY in July even as single-family permits rose to their highest reading since May 2024, confirming builders are completing the existing pipeline while throttling future commitments. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.22% today, up roughly 25 basis points over the past month and 97 basis points higher than a year ago, with the spread to the 10-year Treasury at approximately 2.21 points on today's daily read — well above the historical norm of around 1.7 points, meaning roughly half a point of mortgage rate is spread compression waiting to be released without any Fed action. The MBA purchase index 4-week average was down 4.2% YoY through September 4, a notable step down from the near-flat readings of midsummer, and the 13-week average has fallen for nine consecutive weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA purchase applications by two to four weeks — showed a spot uptick in late June after seven weeks of decline in its 13-week average, but the 13-week MII was still down 2.4% YoY at that point; the divergence between a recovering MII spot read and a still-falling MBA 4-week average is the leading-indicator tension to watch through the end of September. Over the next four weeks, the question is whether the 10-year Treasury yield — at 5.01% today — stabilizes or pushes higher, because at current spread levels, any further Treasury move translates nearly one-for-one into mortgage rates, and 7.5% on the 30-year would test fall demand in a way the spring market didn't face.

The macro backdrop this month is genuinely mixed in ways that matter for housing. The August payroll print — +162,000 versus a +59,000 consensus — removed near-term recession anxiety and likely gave the Fed cover to hold rates steady longer; job growth that outpaces expectations keeps the income side of affordability intact but also reduces urgency to cut. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%, beating the 4.4% consensus, but the long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — rose to 1.93 million, up nearly 9% in a single month and reversing a prior decline; median unemployment duration jumped to 11.4 weeks, up 15% year-over-year. That cohort does not threaten the current selling season, but it is the seed of future delinquency pressure, particularly among borrowers who stretched to buy in 2021–2022 with thin reserves. On inflation, August CPI came in at +3.35% YoY headline and +2.45% YoY core — both cooler than feared — and shelter CPI decelerated slightly to +3.03% YoY, a sign that the lagged rent measurements are finally catching up to real-world softness. Core PCE for July was still running at 3.34% YoY, above the Fed's 2% target, keeping any pivot conversation premature. The 10-year Treasury at 5.01% reflects a market pricing in a higher-for-longer Fed path, and with the 30-year spread still at 2.21 points versus a long-run norm of 1.7, there is a scenario where spread normalization alone — driven by reduced rate volatility and improving bank balance sheets — delivers 30-year rates closer to 6.7–6.8% without a single Fed cut; that is the affordability unlock agents should understand and be able to explain to hesitant buyers.
