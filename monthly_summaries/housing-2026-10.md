# Monthly Housing Summary — October 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held positive year-over-year for the 56th consecutive week on the Altos 13-week average, which sits at +5.5% — a reading that has been improving steadily and tells a more durable story than the headline weekly number. The spot week came in at +5.9% YoY, and the 4-week average is +3.6% YoY, though that average has been decelerating as the weekly series dipped for four straight weeks before the most recent read. Inventory rose for nine consecutive weeks to roughly 1.09 million active listings, up just 0.02% YoY — supply growth has essentially stalled at the national level, which keeps the market tighter than the headlines about rising inventory suggest. New listings are running +1.2% YoY on the 4-week average and +1.5% on the 13-week average, both modest but positive — sellers are returning at a slow, steady pace rather than flooding the market. Pending days on market ticked up 6.2% YoY, a number worth watching; it has been positive for 104 consecutive weeks, meaning homes are taking longer to sell than they were in 2024, though they are not stalling. September existing home sales came in at a 3.99 million SAAR — notably above the 3.85 million consensus — and reversed a recent declining streak, though NSA sales were down 3.9% YoY, a more honest read on underlying demand. New home sales (August, the most current read) printed at 684K SAAR, well above existing sales on a relative basis, continuing the pattern where builders with rate buydowns are capturing a disproportionate share of transaction volume. The 30-year fixed sits at 7.33% as of October 23rd, down only fractionally over the month and up 115 basis points year-over-year. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.34 points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points — and the 4-week average spread has been rising for seven straight weeks, compressing only modestly. If that spread moves back toward 2.0, rates could ease 25-30 basis points without any change in Treasury yields; that is the affordability lever most buyers and sellers are not tracking. The leading indicators, however, are flashing caution: MBA purchase applications fell for seven consecutive weeks, with the 4-week average down 15.7% YoY — the weakest reading since April 2024. The Xactus MII, which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks (it measures mortgage intent signals — credit pulls and pre-qualification activity — upstream of actual application submission), turned negative on its 4-week average at -1.6% YoY after a brief positive run, while the 13-week average has fallen for eight straight weeks. The divergence between the still-positive Altos pending data and the sharply negative MBA applications is the central puzzle heading into November; watch whether the Altos 4-week pending average holds above zero YoY as the seasonal slowdown deepens.

The macro backdrop going into November is more complicated than the soft-landing consensus. September payrolls added only 29,000 jobs against a 59,000 consensus — a meaningful miss — though initial jobless claims for the week of October 17th came in at 194,000, well below the 212,000 expected, suggesting the labor market is softening at the margin without breaking. The housing-relevant mechanism here is straightforward: a buyer who is uncertain about their job does not submit a mortgage application, which is precisely what the MBA decline is reflecting. The long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — rose to 1.944 million in September, up 7.1% YoY and now rising for two consecutive months after a brief dip. This group does not affect the current selling season, but it is the seed population for mortgage stress in six to twelve months; watch it accumulate. On inflation, core PCE ran at 2.95% YoY through August and core CPI came in at 2.57% — both cooler than feared but still above the Fed's 2% target. Shelter CPI at +3.22% YoY continues to lag the real-time rent data: the Zillow ZORI is up only 1.4% YoY and falling for three consecutive months, which means the measured shelter component will continue to pull core CPI lower with a lag — a disinflationary tailwind for rate expectations over the coming quarter. The 10-year Treasury sits at 4.98% today, up nearly a full point year-over-year, which is the single largest constraint on housing affordability right now. The regional picture in the July Case-Shiller data (the most recent available, released in early October) sharpens the two-speed story: Chicago is up 6.8% YoY, New York up 5.7%, Cleveland up 4.2% — Midwest and Northeast metros where supply remains structurally scarce — while Dallas is -0.3%, Denver -1.0%, Las Vegas -1.2%, Seattle -1.6%, and Tampa -0.7% YoY, Sun Belt and West Coast markets still working through the supply overhang from 2022
