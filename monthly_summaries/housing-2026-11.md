**November 2026 Housing Summary**

The Altos weekly demand read through early July showed new pending sales up 5.9% year-over-year on a spot basis, with the four-week average running at +3.6% — a deceleration from the prior trend but still positive, extending the YoY streak to 56 consecutive weeks on the 13-week average, which held at +5.5%. That underlying demand signal matters more than the spot noise. Inventory continued building: Altos total active listings rose 9 weeks straight and sat essentially flat year-over-year at roughly 1.09 million, while new listings were up 8.5% YoY on a spot basis and +1.2% on the four-week average — a meaningful shift from the supply constraint of a year ago. Pending days on market held at 70.8 days, up 6.2% YoY, confirming that buyers are taking more time even as transactions clear. Against that backdrop, October's NAR existing home sales print — 3.75 million SAAR, down 8.8% YoY and 100,000 below consensus — was a deterioration that is hard to soft-pedal: it missed by a full standard deviation on the downside, the West registered an extraordinary -14.5% YoY drop, and the South, which had been the volume engine, turned negative. New home sales (676K SAAR in September, -6.0% YoY) are holding better than existing, in part because builders have been buying down rates, but single-family permits fell 3.9% YoY in September after two months of declines and total starts missed badly — 1,221K vs. 1,350K expected — suggesting builders are pulling back on future pipeline even as they clear completions. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.42% today, up 1.13 points YoY and pressing back toward cycle highs. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.31 points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points — which means roughly 60 basis points of rate relief is locked inside the spread rather than flowing through to buyers. That compression hasn't happened yet. MBA purchase applications fell to a four-week average of 134.0, down 18.5% YoY and at the lowest since January 2024; the 13-week average has fallen 14 consecutive weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index, which leads MBA by two to four weeks, turned its four-week average lower in early July after a brief rise — a signal that the subsequent MBA weakness was telegraphed in advance. Watch whether the MII stabilizes or continues declining into late November; it is the earliest read you have on December contract signings.

The macro picture is genuinely mixed in its housing implications. October payrolls came in at +165,000, well above the 59,000 consensus, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% — both readings that reduce near-term Fed rate-cut probability and help explain why the 10-year Treasury pushed back above 5.1%. A stronger labor market supports buyer qualification, but at 7.4% mortgage rates, qualification and willingness to transact are different things. Core PCE ran at 3.1% year-over-year through September, and October CPI core came in at 2.9% — both above the Fed's 2% target — while average hourly earnings printed hotter than expected at +0.4% month-over-month. That combination keeps the Fed on hold and keeps the 10-year elevated, which flows directly into the mortgage rate that every buyer sees. Shelter CPI eased slightly to +3.5% YoY and continues its lagged descent toward real-time rent data, but it remains above core PCE and will persist as an inflation drag. The regional price story from the August Case-Shiller data (reference period: through August, released October 27) is worth your attention at a listing appointment: Chicago is up 6.6% YoY, New York 6.2%, Cleveland 4.3% — Midwest and Northeast appreciation is genuine and accelerating. Dallas is down 0.7% YoY, Seattle down 2.0%, Tampa down 0.3% and Las Vegas down 1.1% — Sun Belt markets that over-built or over-appreciated remain under pressure on price. If you work those markets, the national median price being up 0.4% YoY is not your number. The one signal to track next month: whether the long-term unemployed cohort — 1.96 million people unemployed 27 weeks or more, up 8.2% YoY — continues to grow. It is not driving distress today, but it accumulates quietly and will shape delinquency volume in 2027.
