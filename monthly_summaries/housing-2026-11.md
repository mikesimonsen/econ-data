# Monthly Housing Summary — November 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

Demand held positive through the four weeks ending November 9, with Altos new pending sales running +5.85% year over year on the spot read and the 4-week average at +3.57% YoY — part of a 56-consecutive-week streak of positive YoY pending sales. The 13-week average is trending in the right direction at +5.48% YoY, which tells you the underlying bid is real, not noise. Inventory on the Realtor.com active count reached 1.18 million in October, up 7.6% YoY and rising for seven straight months, while new listings have softened — down 7.3% YoY in October, their lowest reading since September 2023. That combination (rising active stock, falling new supply) reflects homes sitting longer rather than a fresh wave of sellers entering: Realtor.com median days on market ticked up to 63 days. NAR existing home sales came in at 3.99 million SAAR for September, beating expectations of 3.85 million and reversing two months of declines, though on the NSA count they were still -3.9% YoY — use the YoY read here, not the SAAR uptick. New home sales told a softer story at 676K SAAR, -6.0% YoY, with months supply elevated at 8.4. The 30-year fixed sat at 7.18% as of today, essentially unchanged over the month after a brief dip that reversed — and the spread to the 10-year Treasury is running 2.32 points on the daily read, against a historical norm closer to 1.7 points. That 60-plus basis point excess spread is the affordability story most buyers aren't hearing: if secondary-market conditions normalize, rates fall meaningfully without the Fed moving at all. MBA purchase applications are the concern: the 4-week average is down 18% YoY as of October 30, its weakest reading since February 2024 and falling for six consecutive weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks — had its 4-week average rolling over as well, so there is no early sign yet that applications are about to inflect. Watch the MII and the 10-year over the next four weeks; if the Treasury yield pulls back from 4.86% and the spread begins to compress, that is the setup for a demand rebound heading into the new year.

The macro backdrop is genuinely two-sided. October payrolls came in at +165,000, well above the +59,000 consensus, and unemployment fell to 4.1% — both readings that reduce the near-term probability of Fed rate cuts and kept the 10-year from retreating after the jobs report. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% month over month, slightly ahead of expectations, which keeps Core PCE — running at 3.11% YoY through September, above the Fed's 2% target — from decelerating fast enough to change the rate calculus. Shelter CPI continued its slow grind lower to +3.22% YoY; as that series catches down to real-time rent data (Zillow's ZORI fell 0.82% in September and is up only 1.4% YoY), it will gradually reduce headline inflation pressure, but it is not moving fast enough yet to give the Fed cover. The labor market signal worth watching is not the headline unemployment rate but what is building underneath it: workers unemployed 27 weeks or longer now number 1.96 million, up 8.2% year over year for three consecutive months, and median unemployment duration has risen to 11.9 weeks, up nearly 18% YoY. This cohort does not threaten the current selling season — job-holding owners are not distressed — but it is the leading edge of future mortgage stress if it continues to accumulate. On the regional price story, the August Case-Shiller data (released October 27) shows the Midwest/Northeast vs. Sun Belt divergence still intact: Chicago is up 6.6% YoY, New York up 6.2%, Cleveland up 4.3%, while Dallas is -0.7%, Las Vegas -1.1%, Seattle -2.0%, and Tampa -0.3%. Agents in appreciating markets can defend list prices with that data; agents in Sun Belt metros need to show sellers a different chart. The one macro signal to watch next month: whether the quits rate — which jumped to 2.1% in September after months of softness — sustains its rise. Rising quits signal worker confidence and voluntary job mobility, which historically correlates with household formation and move-up buying. If it holds, it is quietly constructive for spring demand.
