# Monthly Housing Summary — November 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

Demand held positive through October but the leading signals are flashing a warning agents should take seriously before the holiday season locks in. Altos new pending sales came in at 84,611 for the week of July 3 — up 5.9% year-over-year — and the 13-week average remains positive at +5.5% YoY, a streak now running 56 consecutive weeks. That durable streak is the right headline; the concern is what's sitting underneath it. The 4-week average has decelerated to +3.6% YoY and the weekly trend has fallen four straight weeks, so the streak's momentum is fading even if the direction hasn't reversed. Inventory on the Altos read stood at roughly 1.09 million active listings, up just 0.02% year-over-year — supply growth has effectively stalled after nine weeks of weekly gains — while new listings ran +8.5% YoY on the spot print but only +1.2% on the 4-week average, suggesting sellers are testing the market episodically rather than committing in volume. Pending days on market at 70.8 days is running +6.2% above last year, meaning homes that do go under contract are taking longer to get there. NAR existing home sales printed 3.99 million SAAR for September, a slight uptick from August and a 140,000-unit beat versus expectations, though the NSA count was down 3.9% year-over-year — the SAAR beat was real but modest, and the YoY trend is still negative. New home sales diverged sharply, running 676,000 SAAR but down 6.0% year-over-year, with new-home supply at 8.4 months — elevated enough to keep builder pricing defensive, as the median new-home price down 3.9% YoY confirms. The 30-year fixed sits at 7.17% as of November 5, roughly 80 basis points higher than a year ago, and the spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.35 points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points. The spread has actually been widening on the 4-week and 9-week trends, meaning there is no compression benefit accruing to buyers right now; rates are elevated by both Treasury levels and by the extra risk premium lenders are charging. If that spread were to normalize toward 1.7-1.8 points without any move in the 10-year, the 30-year would fall toward 6.5-6.6% — that is the hidden affordability story, still unrealized. The leading indicators are the most urgent thing to watch: the MBA purchase index 4-week average is down 18.0% year-over-year, its weakest reading since February 2024, and the 13-week average has fallen 14 straight weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index — which typically leads MBA applications by two to four weeks by capturing early-stage credit inquiries before borrowers contact a lender — is also softening, with the 13-week average down 1.5% YoY and falling for eight straight weeks. When MII and MBA are both declining in tandem, the signal is more reliable than either alone. Watch whether the MBA 4-week average stabilizes above –20% YoY over the next four weeks; a continued slide would indicate the fall selling season is closing weaker than the pending-sales streak suggests.

The macro picture adds texture to why demand is grinding rather than moving. September payrolls came in at roughly +29,000 — a substantial miss against the +59,000 consensus — and average hourly earnings grew only 0.1% month-over-month, cooler than expected. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2%, though it beat the 4.4% consensus, so the headline read as "less bad" even as the underlying composition was soft. For housing, the most consequential labor-market signal is the long-term unemployed cohort: workers out 27 weeks or more reached 1.944 million in September, up 7.1% year-over-year and rising for two consecutive months after a brief dip. This group does not threaten the current selling season — they are not yet delinquent borrowers — but they are accumulating at a pace that will shape distressed-inventory volume in 2027 if the trend continues. On inflation, Core PCE came in at +3.11% year-over-year in September, still well above the Fed's 2% target and running above core CPI (+2.6%), which limits the Fed's room to cut even as the labor market softens. Shelter CPI at +3.2% YoY is decelerating but sticky, and the lag in how BLS measures rents means shelter will remain elevated in the index longer than actual market rents — Zillow's ZORI fell 0.8% in September alone, now up only 1.4% year-over-year. The rate picture follows: the 10-year Treasury is at 4.82%, up 65 basis points year-over-year, and with Core PCE above 3%, the Fed has limited near-term easing capacity regardless of the weak payroll print.
