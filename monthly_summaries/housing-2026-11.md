**November 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand softened into the fall with meaningful breadth. The Altos 4-week average for new pending sales was running roughly +3.6% YoY as of the week of July 3 — the 56th consecutive week of positive YoY readings on the 13-week average, though that average had flattened from its mid-summer pace. Spot pending came in at +5.9% YoY on the most recent weekly read, but pending days on market have been running about 6% above year-ago levels for two consecutive years, a persistent signal that buyers are taking longer to commit even when they do transact. Inventory on the Altos side was up just fractionally YoY through early July, while Realtor.com active listings were up 7.6% YoY through October — the gap between those two reads reflects timing differences but both point to supply growing faster than demand can absorb it. The October NAR print was the clearest statement of that imbalance: existing home sales came in at a 3.75M SAAR, down 8.8% YoY and 100K units below consensus, with the West falling at a -2.5 standard deviation pace and the South not far behind. Months supply ticked to 5.1, up nearly 16% YoY — not crisis territory, but the trajectory is toward a buyer's market in price-sensitive markets. New home sales (through September) were still running at 676K SAAR, so the builder-to-existing gap, while narrowing, remains a structural feature of this market: builders are moving product that existing owners cannot price to move. On rates, the 30-year fixed sits at 7.36% today, 100 basis points above year-ago levels. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.91%, which puts the mortgage-to-Treasury spread at approximately 2.45 percentage points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points. That spread has been rising for 11 consecutive weeks on the 4-week average. The hidden affordability story here is not the Fed; it is spread compression. If lender risk premiums normalize toward historical levels, the 30-year could fall 50-70 basis points without any change in Treasury yields. MBA purchase applications are running -18.6% YoY on the 4-week average — the worst reading since January 2024 — and the 13-week average has fallen for 14 straight weeks. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index, which leads MBA by 2 to 4 weeks, was already rolling over as of early July, so the MBA weakness currently printing was telegraphed. Watch the MII for a turn: when it stabilizes, MBA applications typically follow within a month. October single-family permits fell to their lowest level since April 2023 and were -12.9% YoY. Starts bounced in October but against a weak base. The divergence — builders still putting shovels in the ground on existing permitted units while pulling back sharply on new permits — means the pipeline tightens in 2027, not 2026.

The macro picture is genuinely two-sided, which makes it harder to tell a clean story at a listing appointment. October payrolls came in at +165K, well above the +59K consensus, and unemployment fell to 4.1% — both better than the market feared. JOLTS shows the quits rate ticking up to 2.1%, which matters for housing because voluntary job-switching correlates with household formation and move-up buying; this was the first meaningful uptick after an extended slide. At the same time, the long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — has risen 8.1% YoY and is three months into a consecutive monthly climb. That cohort does not affect the current selling season, but it is the population most likely to become distressed borrowers in 6 to 12 months if the expansion stalls. On inflation: headline CPI printed at +4.1% YoY in October (cooler than the BofA forecast of +0.9% month-over-month), core CPI at +2.9%, and shelter CPI at +3.5%. Core PCE through September was at +3.1% — above the Fed's target and sticky enough to keep the Fed on hold. The mechanism for housing: until core PCE drifts convincingly toward 2.5%, the Fed has little room to cut, the 10-year stays anchored near 4.9%, and mortgage rates hold above 7% absent spread compression. Regionally, the Case-Shiller August data (released in late October, so covering closings from roughly May through July) shows Chicago at +6.6% YoY and New York at +6.2% — both still appreciating at a pace that makes inventory conversations with sellers straightforward. Meanwhile, Seattle is -2.0%, Dallas -0.7%, Denver -0.8%, Las Vegas -1.1%, and Tampa -0.3% — markets where sellers are often still anchored to 2024 comps.
