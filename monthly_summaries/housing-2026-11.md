# Monthly Housing Summary — November 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The Altos weekly demand read through early July showed new pending sales up 5.9% year-over-year on the spot week, with the four-week average running at +3.6% — a deceleration from the stronger readings earlier in the year but still positive, extending a streak of 56 consecutive weeks of positive YoY pending sales on the 13-week average, which itself held at +5.5%. That is a durable signal, even as the spot and four-week reads have softened. Inventory rose to roughly 1.09 million active listings, up just barely year-over-year — essentially flat — while new listings on a 13-week basis were up about 1.5% YoY, rising for 14 consecutive weeks, suggesting sellers are returning in a slow, steady way rather than flooding the market. Pending days on market ticked up 6.2% YoY, a signal the pace of absorption is modestly slower than a year ago. The October NAR existing home sales print came in at a 3.75 million SAAR, missing the 3.85 million consensus by 100,000 units and landing down 8.8% YoY on the NSA read — the weakest October since 2023 — with the West (-14.5% YoY NSA) and South (-10.8%) the softest regions. Months supply climbed to 5.1, up nearly 16% YoY and accelerating, which is not an inventory-crisis number but is notably above the 4.0–4.5 range that tends to hold prices flat. New home sales (676K SAAR through September) are holding better than existing — builders are still clearing pipeline even as single-family starts fell 4.6% and total permits turned negative YoY for the first time in a while, with single-family permits off 3.9% YoY; the starts-vs-permits divergence suggests builders are finishing what they started but pulling back on new commitments, which matters more for 2027 supply than the current selling season. On rates: the 30-year fixed sits at 7.50% today, up roughly 25 basis points over the past month and 116 basis points above a year ago, while the 10-year Treasury has risen to 5.15%. The mortgage-Treasury spread is 2.35 points — still well above the historical norm of around 1.7 points, meaning the hidden affordability story is that lenders, not the Fed, hold the lever; if the spread compresses 25–30 basis points from here, the 30-year falls meaningfully without any Fed action. MBA purchase applications are down 18.5% YoY on the four-week average, the worst reading since early 2024, and the 13-week average has been falling for 14 consecutive weeks — that is the number to watch, because the Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (a credit-inquiry-based leading indicator that tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks) turned positive YoY on the spot week in early July before its four-week average rolled back over, suggesting the MBA improvement has not yet arrived and the signal is at best mixed. Watch whether the MII four-week average firms over the next four weeks; if it does, purchase applications should follow into December.

The macro backdrop is running hotter than the housing data implies it should be. October payrolls came in at +165,000 — nearly triple the 59,000 consensus — and the unemployment rate fell 10 basis points to 4.1%, well below the 4.4% expected. That is a labor market that is not breaking, which supports the buyer pool in theory, but average hourly earnings came in at +0.4% month-over-month and +3.1% YoY, keeping the Fed on hold and contributing to the rate pressure agents are feeling at every listing appointment. Core PCE through September was +3.1% YoY — still above the Fed's 2% target — and October CPI shelter held at +3.5% YoY; shelter inflation is slowing but still high enough to keep core readings elevated for several more months given the lag between market rents and the BLS measure. The mechanism is straightforward: until shelter CPI falls closer to 2.5%, core PCE stays above target, the Fed stays cautious, and the 10-year stays elevated, keeping the 30-year near or above 7.5%. The long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 or more weeks — is up 8.2% YoY and has risen for three consecutive months; that is not a today problem for sellers, but it is the leading edge of future delinquency pressure in 12–18 months if it continues to accumulate. Regionally, the Case-Shiller August data (released in late October, so reflecting conditions two to three months ago) makes the two-speed market explicit: Chicago +6.6% YoY, New York +6.2%, Cleveland +4.3%, Miami +4.3% — all appreciating — while Dallas is down 0.7%, Denver down 0.8%, Las Vegas down 1.1%, Seattle down 2.0%, and Tampa down 0.3% for a 22nd consecutive negative YoY month.
