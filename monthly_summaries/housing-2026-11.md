**November 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand softened meaningfully heading into November. Altos weekly new pending sales came in at +5.85% YoY for the week ending July 3 (the most recent Altos read in the snapshot), with the four-week average at +3.57% YoY — decelerating but still positive, extending a streak of 56 consecutive weeks of positive YoY pending sales on the 13-week average, which held at +5.48%. That streak matters: it tells you the underlying transaction count has been running ahead of last year for over a year, even as individual weeks wobble. What is worth watching is the direction — the four-week average has been fading, and the 13-week average is essentially flat, suggesting the YoY cushion is thinning. Altos inventory tracked just over 1.08 million units, up roughly 0.02% YoY — essentially flat — with new listings running +8.46% YoY on the spot week but only +1.22% on the four-week average, meaning the listings pop is noisy rather than a durable acceleration. Pending days on market held at 70.8 days, up 6.2% YoY, confirming homes are sitting longer than a year ago. NAR existing home sales for October came in at a 3.75 million SAAR — below the 3.85 million consensus, down 8.76% YoY on the NSA count, and at the lowest pace since October 2023. The West was notably weak, off 14.5% YoY NSA. New home sales (September, 676K SAAR) are holding in comparably better, supported by builder incentives, but single-family permits fell 3.9% YoY in September after turning negative for the first time in several months — an early signal that builders are pulling back on future pipeline even as they clear existing starts. The rate environment is the driver: the 30-year fixed is at 7.50% today, up 116 basis points YoY, with the 10-year Treasury at 5.15%. The mortgage-Treasury spread — the gap between what lenders charge and the underlying benchmark, historically around 1.7 percentage points — sits at roughly 2.33 points on the four-week average, still elevated but not yet compressing in a helpful direction; it actually ticked back up this week. That spread is the hidden affordability lever: it doesn't require Fed action to narrow, but right now it is widening again, which removes a source of potential rate relief. MBA purchase applications are down 18.5% YoY on the four-week average, the weakest reading since January 2024 — though the most recent weekly print bounced +7.5%, worth monitoring to see if that holds. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (a credit-inquiry-based measure that tends to lead MBA by two to four weeks) turned positive YoY as of early July but its four-week and 13-week averages were both falling at that point, suggesting the bounce in MBA applications may not be durable. Watch the next two Xactus prints closely — if the MII firms, the MBA uptick is real; if it rolls over, November closings will confirm what October already implied.

The macro picture complicates the rate story. October payrolls came in at +165,000, well above the 59,000 consensus — a genuine beat that briefly firmed the 10-year yield, which is now at 5.15%, a 104-basis-point increase YoY. Higher long-end yields directly pull the 30-year mortgage rate up with them, reinforcing the affordability headwind. Headline CPI for October was +4.08% YoY, though the monthly print of +0.3% came in well below the 0.9% BofA forecast, suggesting at least some deceleration in the pace of price increases — markets took that as a mild positive. Core PCE through September ran at +3.11% YoY, still above the Fed's 2% target, which argues against near-term easing. Shelter CPI continues to run at +3.54% YoY, but that series lags actual rent conditions by roughly 12-18 months; the Zillow rent index (ZORI) is only up 1.42% YoY as of September and fell for three consecutive months — so shelter inflation in CPI will continue drifting lower, which eventually gives the Fed more room. The labor market's surface numbers look resilient, but underneath them the long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — has risen 8.2% YoY and is now at 1.963 million, up three consecutive months. This group does not threaten the current selling season, but it is the population most likely to face mortgage stress in a 6-to-12-month window. On the regional price picture, the August Case-Shiller data (the most recent available, covering a two-month lag) shows Chicago at +6.6% YoY and New York at +6.2% — both still appreciating meaningfully — while Dallas is -0.68% YoY, Las Vegas -1.06%, Seattle -2.01%, and Tampa -0.
