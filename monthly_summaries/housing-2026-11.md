# Monthly Housing Summary — November 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

New pending sales held in positive year-over-year territory for the 56th consecutive week through early October, with the 13-week average running +5.5% YoY — a durable trend that the weekly noise has not broken. The four-week average softened to +3.6% YoY, worth watching but not yet alarming given where we are in the seasonal slowdown. Altos active inventory reached roughly 1.09 million units, up fractionally year-over-year — supply growth has essentially stalled against the seasonal norm that typically pushes inventory higher through fall. New listings on a 13-week basis were up about +1.5% YoY, rising for 14 consecutive weeks, which is a healthy but modest replenishment rate. Pending days on market ticked to 70.8 days, +6.2% YoY, confirming that the market is absorbing homes a bit more slowly than a year ago even as demand holds positive. NAR existing home sales printed at 3.99 million SAAR for September, slightly above the 3.85 million consensus, with the NSA count down 3.9% YoY — the SAAR beat mostly reflects favorable seasonal adjustment rather than a step-change in activity. New home sales came in at 676,000 SAAR, down 6.0% YoY, with months supply at 8.4 — builders are carrying more finished and under-construction inventory than they would prefer, and single-family permits fell 3.9% YoY in September, the first year-over-year decline after a string of positive readings. That permit signal matters: starts are already running down sharply in the West (-25% YoY) and Midwest (-20% YoY), while the South held a +10% YoY gain, and the retreat in permits suggests the starts gap will persist rather than close. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.30% as of today, up roughly 25 basis points over the past month and 96 basis points above a year ago. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.31 points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points — meaning roughly 60 basis points of the rate burden is spread premium rather than benchmark yield. That spread has actually been drifting wider over the past nine weeks, which is the opposite of the compression story agents were watching earlier this year. The MBA purchase index four-week average is -18.0% YoY, at its weakest since February 2024, and the 13-week average has fallen for 14 consecutive weeks. The Xactus MII, which typically leads MBA by two to four weeks, was showing modest positive YoY readings in early October but its four-week average had already rolled over — the subsequent weakness in MBA is exactly what that divergence implied. Over the next four weeks, watch whether the MII stabilizes or continues lower; that will tell you whether the MBA weakness is temporary rate-shock repricing or the start of a more sustained demand retreat heading into 2027 spring setup season.

The October jobs report landed well above consensus — +165,000 payrolls against a +59,000 estimate, and unemployment fell to 4.1% when the street was expecting 4.4%. That is unambiguously supportive of housing fundamentals in the near term: employed households form households, and the JOLTS data added texture, with the quits rate rising to 2.1% in September, a leading signal that workers still feel confident enough to change jobs voluntarily. For housing, a rising quits rate historically precedes household formation and geographic mobility six to twelve months out. The complication is inflation: headline CPI printed +4.1% YoY in October, core PCE was +3.1% YoY through September, and average hourly earnings came in at +3.1% YoY — the wage-inflation relationship means the Fed has little room to ease, and the 10-year Treasury at 4.99% today reflects exactly that constraint. Shelter CPI continued at +3.5% YoY, still running above the pace that would allow core PCE to converge toward 2%, which keeps the Fed on hold and keeps the 10-year elevated. The regional price divergence visible in the August Case-Shiller data is worth naming clearly: Chicago is running +6.6% YoY, New York +6.2%, Cleveland +4.3%, and Miami +4.3% — markets where supply is structurally constrained and affordability is stretched on a dollar basis but not on a relative basis for local buyers. Meanwhile Dallas is -0.7% YoY (17 consecutive months negative), Seattle -2.0% (13 months negative), Las Vegas -1.1% (12 months negative), and Tampa -0.3% (22 months negative). The Sun Belt correction is slow but persistent, and the national index at +2.2% YoY obscures it. The one macro signal to watch next month is whether the long-term unemployed cohort — currently 1.96 million persons unemployed 27 weeks or more, up 8.2% YoY and rising for three consecutive months — continues to accumulate.
