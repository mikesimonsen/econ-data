# Monthly Housing Summary — November 2026

*For Compass International Holdings. Prompt source: `prompts/monthly_housing_summary.txt`.*

---

The Altos weekly demand read through early July shows new pending sales running 5.85% ahead of a year ago on the spot week, with the four-week average at +3.57% YoY — a deceleration from the pace earlier in the cycle but still positive. The 13-week average has held a YoY gain for 56 consecutive weeks, and that average continues to trend higher, which is the more durable signal to carry into listing conversations. Inventory stood just above 1.08 million active listings, essentially flat YoY (+0.02%), with new listings up 8.46% on the spot week and the 13-week new-listings average running +1.5% YoY — supply is trickling in faster than it was, but not fast enough to pressure prices at the national level. Pending days on market at 70.8 days are running 6% above last year's pace, so absorption has softened at the margin even as the direction of pending sales remains positive. NAR's September existing home sales came in at a 3.99 million SAAR, a small beat against the 3.85 million consensus and an uptick from August — though on the NSA YoY basis that agents should use for direction, sales are still down 3.9% from a year ago. New home sales at 676,000 SAAR are running down nearly 6% YoY, with 8.4 months of new-home supply suggesting builders remain in clearing mode. The 30-year fixed is 7.30% as of today, up nearly a full percentage point from a year ago, and the spread to the 10-year Treasury sits at 2.35 points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 points. That gap is the hidden affordability lever: if lenders and bond markets normalize the spread even partially, the 30-year rate can fall meaningfully without any Fed action. Watch the spread, not just the headline rate. For the coming month, the MBA purchase index four-week average is off 16.8% YoY and sitting at its lowest since February 2024 — a sharp deterioration. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index, which leads MBA applications by two to four weeks, turned its four-week average lower in early July after a brief rise, with the 13-week average down 1.5% YoY and falling for eight consecutive weeks. The MII was not signaling a recovery ahead; the MBA weakness that followed was not a surprise. Single-family permits fell 3.9% YoY in September after two consecutive months of declines, while starts fell 4.6% on the month — builders are delivering existing pipeline (single-family completions remain elevated) but pulling back on future authorizations. That matters for 2027 supply, not the current selling season, but it is worth noting to sellers who worry about competition from new construction.

The macro backdrop is tightening around the housing market in ways that deserve more attention than the headlines have given them. September payrolls came in at roughly 29,000 — a substantial miss against the 59,000 consensus — and average hourly earnings grew only 0.1% month over month against an expected 0.3%, both of which pushed rate-cut expectations forward in time. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2%, beating the 4.4% forecast, but the composition of the labor market is worth watching: the long-term unemployed (27-plus weeks) rose for a second consecutive month and are now up 7.1% YoY, and the median duration of unemployment is running 13.9% above last year's pace. These households are not yet missing mortgage payments, but they represent the future delinquency pipeline — agents who work distressed listings should start tracking this cohort. On the inflation side, Core PCE came in at 3.11% YoY in September, still above the Fed's target and rising for seven consecutive months — that is the number keeping the Fed cautious, and the 10-year Treasury at 4.95% today (up 82 basis points YoY) reflects that caution. The mortgage-Treasury spread at 2.35 points compressing even modestly would do more for monthly payments than the next 25 basis points of Fed cuts. The regional price picture from Case-Shiller (through August, the data's typical two-month lag) confirms the two-speed market: Chicago is up 6.6% YoY, New York up 6.2%, Cleveland up 4.3%, Miami up 4.3% — markets with constrained supply and strong household formation. Dallas is down 0.7% YoY, Denver down 0.8%, Seattle down 2.0%, Las Vegas down 1.1%, Tampa down 0.3% — Sun Belt markets still working through the inventory overhang built in 2022-2023. Agents in those two camps are having fundamentally different price conversations, and the data supports that divergence continuing. The one signal to watch next month: October payrolls, due in early November. If the labor market confirms September's softness rather than revises it away, the case for rate relief strengthens — and with the spread elevated, even a modest rally in Treasuries would translate more directly into lower borrowing costs than it has historically.
