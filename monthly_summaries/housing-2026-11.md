**November 2026 Housing Summary**

Demand held positive but decelerated meaningfully heading into autumn. The Altos weekly new pending sales read for the week of July 3 came in at 84,611, up 5.85% year-over-year, extending a streak of positive YoY readings to 56 consecutive weeks on the 13-week average — but the 4-week average has slowed to +3.57% YoY and the spot week itself fell for a fourth consecutive week. That deceleration is real, not seasonal noise. Inventory stood at roughly 1.09 million active listings as of early July, essentially flat year-over-year (+0.02%), while new listings rose 8.46% YoY on the spot week — the 13-week new-listings average has now risen for 14 consecutive weeks, up 1.5% YoY, which keeps supply growing modestly but not flooding the market. Pending days on market at 70.8 days are running 6.2% above year-ago levels, a consistent signal that homes are sitting longer. The NAR September existing home sales print of 3.99 million SAAR beat expectations of 3.85 million and snapped a string of declines, though the NSA YoY reading came in at -3.9% — the SAAR beat reflects favorable seasonal adjustment more than raw transaction momentum. New home sales at 676,000 SAAR in September ran nearly 6% below year-ago pace, and with 8.4 months of new home supply on hand, builders are sitting on elevated finished-lot inventory even as single-family permits have now turned negative YoY. That starts-vs.-permits divergence — single-family starts down from their recent high while permits fall nearly 4% YoY — tells you builders are completing the pipeline they already committed to but pulling back on future authorizations, a supply constraint that matters more for 2028 than for the current selling season. The 30-year fixed rate sits at 7.30% as of November 10, up nearly a full percentage point year-over-year and roughly 12 basis points higher just over the past two days. The spread to the 10-year Treasury is 2.31 percentage points — well above the historical norm of roughly 1.7 percentage points — and the 4-week average spread has risen for nine consecutive weeks to 2.32 percentage points. That elevated spread is the hidden affordability story: rates could fall 50-60 basis points from pure spread normalization alone, without any Fed movement. The MBA purchase index for the week of November 6 printed 141.2 — a week-over-week uptick that breaks a recent string of declines — but the 4-week average at 134.0 is down 18.5% YoY, the weakest reading since January 2024. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index (a credit-inquiry-based series that leads MBA by roughly 2-4 weeks) turned positive YoY as of early July but its 13-week average has fallen for eight consecutive weeks — suggesting the MBA uptick this week may be a one-week bounce rather than a turn. Watch whether the MII 4-week average stabilizes over the next four weeks; if it doesn't, expect the MBA purchase series to soften again through late November.

The macro picture is genuinely mixed in a way that matters for housing. October payrolls came in at +165,000, well above the 59,000 consensus estimate, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% against an expected 4.4% — on the surface, a strong labor market supports housing demand. But the composition is worth examining: average hourly earnings rose 0.4% month-over-month (hotter than the 0.3% expected), and the 10-year Treasury yield has risen to 5.00% in response to the combined signal of solid payrolls and sticky wages. That yield is the direct input to the mortgage rate, and at 5.00% it keeps the 30-year fixed anchored above 7% even if the spread were to compress. Core PCE for September ran 3.11% year-over-year — above the Fed's 2% target — and October CPI shelter held at +3.54% YoY, decelerating but not yet cooling fast enough to give the Fed cover to ease. The mechanism is straightforward: until shelter inflation falls further, core PCE stays elevated, the Fed holds, the 10-year stays bid, and mortgage rates remain in the 7-plus range. The long-term unemployed cohort — those out of work 27 weeks or more — reached 1.963 million in October, up 8.15% year-over-year and rising for three consecutive months. This is not a 2026 selling-season problem; it becomes a distressed-borrower and delinquency story in 6-12 months if it keeps accumulating.
